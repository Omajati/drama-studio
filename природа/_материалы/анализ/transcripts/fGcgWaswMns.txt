Why You'd Never Want To Meet A Sperm Whale | 599 s
Picture  this.  You're  floating  in  the open  ocean  and  you've  done  your homework.  Sharks  are  rare  out  here. Orcas  tend  to  leave  divers  alone. Nothing  in  the  sea  is  actually  looking for  you.  That's  the  rule. Then  you  feel  it.  Not  see  it,  feel  it.  A deep  knocking  in  your  chest  like  someone tapping  on  your  ribs  from  the  inside. That's  a  sperm  whale  and  it  already knows  exactly  where  you  are. When  most  people  think  of  a  dangerous whale,  they  think  of  orcas,  the  ones with  killer  right  there  in  the  name.  Or they  picture  a  humpback  breaching  and landing  on  a  boat.  And  yes,  orcas  are apex  predators  and  big  whales  have flipped  boats  by  accident.  But  the  sperm whale  is  in  a  different  category.  It's the  biggest  predator  with  teeth  that  has ever  lived.  It  hunts  in  places  no  other mammal  can  reach  and  it  once  sank  a  ship on  purpose. So  let's  talk  about  why  you'd  never  want to  be  in  the  water  with  one. First,  the  size  because  it's  hard  to A  big  male  sperm  whale  can  reach  around 16  m  long  and  weigh  roughly  40  tons. That's  about  the  length  of  a  city  bus and  the  weight  of  six  or  seven elephants.  And  it  looks  like  this. That  giant  square  block  at  the  front isn't  a  forehead.  It's  a  head  that  takes up  around  a  third  of  the  whole  animal. Down  at  the  bottom  of  it,  there's  a  long narrow  jaw  that  looks  almost  too  small for  the  rest  of  the  body. But  just  look  at  those  teeth.  They  only grow  on  the  bottom  jaw  and  each  one  can be  about  as  long  as  your  hand  and  weigh around  a  kilogram. A  humpback  filters  tiny  krill  through  a mouth  full  of  bristles.  A  sperm  whale doesn't  filter  anything.  It  grabs. Which  raises  the  obvious  question,  what does  something  this  big  actually  eat? Squid.  And  not  small  ones.  Sperm  whales hunt  in  the  deep,  dark  water  where  giant squid  and  colossal  squid  live.  And  if you've  been  watching  Depth  Theory  for  a while,  you  already  know  what  those  squid are  carrying.  Hooks,  suckers  lined  with teeth,  beaks  that  can  slice  through The  sperm  whale  goes  down  there  anyway, on  purpose,  every  single  day.  And  it comes  back  up  with  proof. Sperm  whales  are  often  covered  in  round scars  and  long  scratches  from  squid fighting  back.  Their  stomachs  can  hold thousands  of  squid  beaks  because  beaks don't  digest. A  sperm  whale  basically  keeps  a  receipt for  every  meal  it's  ever  had.  Most  of  us can't  even  find  one  receipt  when  we  need To  get  there,  it  does  something  no  other mammal  can  really  match.  A  sperm  whale can  dive  more  than  2  km  down  and  hold its  breath  for  over  an  hour.  You'd struggle  to  hold  yours  for  1  minute  in  a swimming  pool.  And  the  deeper  it  goes, the  darker  it  gets  until  there's  no light  at  all.  So,  how  does  it  find anything  down  there?  With  sound.  That huge  head  is  basically  a  biological speaker.  Inside  it  sits  a  massive  organ filled  with  a  waxy  oil  called spermaceti.  And  the  whale  uses  it  to focus  clicks  into  a  beam,  like  a flashlight  made  of  sound.  Those  clicks bounce  off  squid,  fish,  rocks,  and  you, and  come  back  as  a  picture  of  everything around  it.  And  these  aren't  gentle clicks.  The  sperm  whale  is  officially the  loudest  animal  on  Earth.  Its  clicks have  been  measured  at  around  236 decibels  underwater.  Some  of  that  sound can  carry  for  tens  of  kilometers.  Divers who've  been  close  to  sperm  whales describe  feeling  the  clicks  hitting their  bodies,  rattling  straight  through their  chest.  Remember  that  knocking  from the  start?  That  was  you  being  scanned. Back  in  the  '80s,  some  scientists  even suggested  sperm  whales  might  be  able  to stun  prey  with  a  blast  of  sound.  That idea  has  never  been  proven.  But honestly,  the  fact  that  scientists  had to  sit  down  and  seriously  consider  it says  a  lot  about  how  loud  these  things are.  But  here's  the  part  that  makes  the sperm  whale  genuinely  unsettling.  It's not  just  big  and  loud,  it's  smart.  It has  the  largest  brain  of  any  animal  that has  ever  lived,  around  8  kg.  That's heavier  than  a  bowling  ball,  and  it  uses that  brain  to  talk.  Sperm  whales  live  in close  family  groups  and  communicate  with patterns  of  clicks  called  codas.  In 2024,  researchers  studying  families  off the  coast  of  Dominica  analyzed  nearly 9,000  of  these  codas  and  found  that  the whales  combine  rhythm,  tempo,  and  tiny timing  changes  in  a  way  that  works  a  bit like  an  alphabet.  Different  groups  even have  their  own  dialects.  So,  picture  it again.  You're  in  the  water  and  a  40-ton animal  is  scanning  you  with  sound  and then  clicking  something  to  its  family about  what  it  found.  I'd  really  like  to know  what  it  said.  Actually,  no,  I probably  wouldn't.  But  all  of  this raises  an  obvious  question.  Has  a  sperm whale  ever  actually  turned  that  size  and intelligence  against  people?  Yes,  once, very  famously.  In  November  1820,  a whaling  ship  called  the  Essex  was hunting  sperm  whales  in  the  Pacific Ocean  when  a  huge  male  turned  around  and rammed  it.  Then  it  came  back  and  rammed it  again.  The  ship  sank  and  the  crew ended  up  drifting  across  the  ocean  in small  boats  for  months.  Their  story became  the  inspiration  for  Moby  Dick. And  the  Essex  wasn't  the  only  one.  In 1851,  the  same  year  Moby  Dick  was published,  another  whaling  ship  called the  Anne  Alexander  was  also  rammed  and sunk  by  a  sperm  whale. Some  scientists  have  even  argued  that the  giant  head  might  work  partly  like  a battering  ram,  something  males  could  use when  fighting  each  other.  Ships  just happened  to  be  the  wrong  shape  to  argue To  be  fair  to  the  whale,  the  crew  had come  out  there  to  kill  it  and  its family.  So,  that  wasn't  really  an attack,  it  was  a  complaint.  I  know  the question  we're  all  thinking.  If  you  were in  the  water  and  a  sperm  whale  came straight  at  you,  what  would  you  actually Well,  let's  break  it  down.  You  could swim  away,  but  a  sperm  whale  cruises comfortably  at  a  few  kilometers  an  hour and  can  sprint  much  faster.  And  it's been  tracking  you  with  sound  since before  you  even  saw  it.  That's  like trying  to  sneak  out  of  a  room  that's already  watching  you. You  could  dive  down  and  hide.  Remember, this  animal  hunts  2  km  below  you  in total  darkness.  Going  deeper  isn't hiding.  That's  walking  into  its  kitchen. You  could  stay  perfectly  still.  The problem  is  the  sperm  whale  doesn't  need you  to  move.  It  doesn't  see  you.  It hears  you  right  down  to  the  air  inside your  lungs. You  could  try  to  scare  it  off.  And  look, I'm  not  saying  I'd  have  a  plan,  but  I'm pretty  sure  splashing  at  the  loudest animal  on  Earth  isn't  going  to  win  the volume  contest.  So,  the  honest  verdict is  this.  If  a  sperm  whale  decided  you were  a  problem,  there's  not  a  single thing  you  could  do  about  it.  It's  really easy  to  look  at  all  this  and  assume sperm  whales  are  deep-sea  monsters.  But here's  the  surprising  part.  They  almost never  come  after  people  at  all.  There's no  pattern  of  sperm  whales  hunting humans.  In  places  like  Dominica,  small groups  of  permitted  swimmers  and researchers  spend  time  in  the  water  near them,  and  the  whales  mostly  treat  them with  a  kind  of  calm  curiosity.  A  sperm whale  wants  you  gone  about  as  much  as you  want  a  fly  gone.  It  just  isn't  that interested.  Even  their  downtime  is strange.  Sperm  whales  sleep  in  short naps,  floating  straight  up  and  down  in the  water  like  giant  motionless  logs.  A whole  family  can  hang  there  in  silence for  a  few  minutes  at  a  time.  It's honestly  one  of  the  eeriest  things  you could  ever  swim  into.  The  real  danger has  always  gone  the  other  way.  Whalers hunted  sperm  whales  for  their  oil  for centuries.  By  one  widely  cited estimate,  there  were  about  1.1  million sperm  whales  before  commercial  whaling, and  only  around  a  third  of  that  number by  the  end  of  the  20th  century. In  the  1960s,  whalers  were  killing  up  to 25,000  of  them  a  year. Commercial  whaling  largely  stopped  after a  ban  in  the  '80s,  but  the  problems didn't  stop  with  it.  Today,  sperm  whales get  hit  by  ships,  tangled  in  fishing gear,  and  confused  by  underwater  noise. Some  have  washed  up  with  stomachs  full of  plastic  bags  and  fishing  nets.  And  in places  like  Alaska  and  the  Southern Ocean,  sperm  whales  have  learned  to  pull fish  straight  off  longline  fishing hooks,  which  means  the  sperm  whale  and the  colossal  squid  have  something  in common.  They  both  got  caught  stealing someone's  dinner. It's  easy  to  see  the  sperm  whale  as  a monster,  but  this  animal  holds  the  ocean together  in  ways  most  people  never  hear about.  When  sperm  whales  feed  in  the deep  and  then  come  up  to  breathe,  they release  their  waste  near  the  surface. And  that  waste  is  rich  in  iron.  That iron  feeds  tiny  plankton,  and  those plankton  pull  carbon  out  of  the One  study  estimated  that  sperm  whales  in the  Southern  Ocean  remove  around  240,000 tons  of  carbon  every  single  year,  more than  they  release. So,  the  biggest  predator  with  teeth  on the  planet  is  also,  in  a  weird  way, gardening  the  ocean. We  tell  ourselves  nothing  in  the  sea  is really  looking  for  us.  And  honestly, that's  mostly  true.  But  somewhere  out there,  2  km  down  in  the  dark,  something with  the  biggest  brain  on  Earth  is listening,  talking,  and  remembering.
