Check  this.  For  the  entirety  of  modern history,  there  has  been  a  very  simple rule.  We  hunt  the  animals.  The  animals do  not  hunt  us.  We've  built  cities, locked  our  doors,  and  completely  removed ourselves  from  the  food  chain. Out  of  the  millions  of  species on  the  planet,  there  are  exactly  four animals  that  still  look  at  a  human  being and  see  us  as  prey. When  a  hippo  bites  the  boat  in half  and  kills  the  people  inside,  they aren't  looking  for  a  snack.  Hippos  are just  violently  territorial  and essentially  hate  everything  that breathes  near  them.  They  want  you  out  of their  river,  and  killing  you  is  just  the fastest  way  to  achieve  that.  Which, honestly,  is  a  pretty  extreme  way  to  ask someone  to  get  off  your  lawn.  Great white  sharks  are  a  bit  different,  but the  rule  still  applies.  When  a  shark bites  a  surfer,  it's  almost  always  a case  of  mistaken  identity  or  simple curiosity.  Sharks  don't  have  hands,  so they  investigate  things  by  putting  them in  their  mouths.  If  a  15-ft  great  white shark  actually  wanted  to  eat  you,  you wouldn't  get  a  shark  bite  and  live  to tell  the  story.  You'd  just  be  gone.  The animals  on  this  list  don't  care  about their  territory,  and  they  aren't confused. So,  if  you  want  to  find  an  animal  that genuinely  wants  to  digest  you,  you  have to  start  in  the  most  unforgiving environment  on  the  planet.  If  you  happen to  find  yourself  stranded  out  in  the Arctic  ice,  the  freezing  temperatures are  actually  the  least  of  your  worries. Your  real  problem is  the  polar  bear.  Polar  bears  are  the only  land  mammals  on  Earth  that  will catch  the  scent  of  a  human,  actively track  them  for  miles  across  the  ice,  and attempt  to  eat  them.  To  understand  just how  crazy  this  is,  you  have  to  compare them  to  their  closest  relatives.  If  you accidentally  surprise  a  grizzly  bear  in the  woods,  it  will  probably  charge  you. But  grizzlies  almost  always  acting defensively.  They're  protecting  their cubs,  or  they're  protecting  a  carcass they  just  killed.  If  you  play  dead,  a grizzly  will  usually  realize  you  aren't a  threat  anymore,  leave  you  alone,  and walk  away.  A  grizzly  bear  attacks  you because  it  wants  you  to  leave.  A  polar bear  attacks  you  because  it  wants  you  to stay.  As  in,  stay  in  its  stomach.  If that  wasn't  clear.  If  you  play  dead  in front  of  a  polar  bear,  you're essentially  just  doing  the  prep  work  for them.  You're  basically  just  plating yourself  like  a  five-star  meal.  How polite.  They  don't  care  if  you're  a threat.  They'll  smell  you  from  20  miles away,  lock  onto  your  location,  and  just start  walking  towards  you.  And  they won't  stop  until  they  find  you.  It's basically  the  world's  worst  game  of  hide and  seek.  Except  the  seeker  weighs  a thousand  pounds,  and you  don't  get  to  go  home  after.  But  why are  polar  bears  so  uniquely  aggressive towards  us  when  every  other  bear  species on  Earth  tries  to  avoid  us?  The  reason polar  bears  hunt  humans  is  basically just  because  of  where  they  live.  And  the fact  that  they've  been  completely isolated  from  us.  For  millions  of  years, polar  bears  evolved  in  an  environment completely  devoid  of  human  civilization. While  wolves,  tigers,  and  brown  bears spent  thousands  of  years  learning  that humans  carry  spears,  build  fires,  and shoot  guns,  polar  bears  were  entirely isolated  at  the  top  of  the  world.  They have  absolutely  zero  genetic  memory  of humans  being  a  threat.  Because  they never  learned  to  fear  us,  they  just don't  see  us  as  a  dominant  species. Combine  that  with  the  lack  of  fear  from the  fact  they  live  in  a  frozen  wasteland where  food  is  unbelievably  scarce.  In the  Arctic,  you  simply  cannot  afford  to be  picky.  If  something  is  made  of  meat, and  it's  moving,  a  polar  bear  is  going to  try  and  eat  it.  They  basically  just see  us  as  a  very  weak,  very  slow,  and very  loud  seal  that  decided  to  walk  on land.  But  tracking  a  human  for  20  miles across  the  ice  takes  a  massive  amount  of energy.  So  some  predators  don't  chase you  at  all.  They  just  wait  for  you  to come  to  them.  If  you  leave  the  freezing Arctic  and  head  down  to  the  tropical rivers  of  Africa,  or  the  coastlines  of Australia,  you'll  find  the  second  animal on  our  list,  the  crocodile. Specifically,  the  Nile  crocodile  and  the saltwater  crocodile.  And  if  you're wondering  why  they're  so  dangerous, well,  just  look  at  those  jaws.  Yeah, you're  not  surviving  that.  A  crocodile is  never  going  to  catch  your  scent  and track  you  for  miles  across  the  savanna. They  just  treat  humans  exactly  the  same way  they  treat  a  zebra,  a  wildebeest,  or a  wild  boar.  They  draw  a  hard  line  at the  water's  edge.  If  you  stay  on  the land,  you're  fine.  But  the  second  you step  a  single  toe  into  their  water, you're  no  longer  the  apex  predator. You're  immediately  placed  on  the  menu. Crocodiles  don't  care  about  human dominance.  They've  been  using  the  exact same  hunting  strategy  since  the  time  of the  dinosaurs,  and  they  have  no  reason to  change  it  just  because  we  learned  how to  build  cities.  But  the  truly terrifying  part  about  crocodiles  is  how they  actually  plan  their  attacks.  People tend  to  look  at  reptiles  as  these  slow, primitive  eating  machines  that  just blindly  snap  at  whatever  floats  by.  But crocodiles  are  actually  highly intelligent,  and  they're  arguably  the most  patient  predators  on  earth.  They're actively  studying  your  routines.  If  a crocodile  notices  that  a  specific  person comes  to  the  river  to  wash  clothes, collect  water,  or  fish  at  the  exact  same time  every  single  day,  the  crocodile will  memorize  that  pattern.  It  will  swim to  that  specific  spot,  sink  just  below the  surface,  and  wait.  They  have  these little  bumps  on  their  faces  called  ISOs that  basically  let  them  feel  water moving  around  them.  They  don't  even  need to  open  their  eyes  to  know  exactly  where you're  standing.  They'll  wait  for  days if  they  have  to,  just  sitting  there  in the  mud,  doing  absolutely  nothing. Honestly,  you  have  to  respect  the commitment.  And  when  the  human  lays  down and  is  completely  vulnerable,  the crocodile  strikes  in  a  fraction  of  a second,  dragging  them  underwater  to drown.  This  is  just  a  straight-up assassination.  But  crocodiles  and  polar bears  are  both  operating  on  pure biological  instinct.  For  other  animals, hunting  animals  is  an  actual  learned cultural  tradition.  This  brings  us  to the  third  animal,  the  tiger. It's  a  brutal environment  filled  with  deep  mud, shifting  tides,  and  dense  roots. Locals  constantly  have  to  enter the  forest  to  fish,  cut  wood,  or  collect honey.  And  the  tigers  know  this.  Instead of  avoiding  the  boats,  the  Sundarban tigers  will  actively  stalk  the  workers. And look,  I'm  not  saying  I'd  do  well  in  the wild,  but  if  I'm  on  a  boat,  the  last thing  I'm  expecting  is  a  400-lb  cat  to do  a  stealth  boarding  maneuver.  And  no, we  didn't  just  find  a  few  rogue  tigers making  a  mistake.  This  is  an  entire population  of  predators  that  have realized  humans  are  an  incredibly  easy food  source,  and  they  have  passed  that knowledge  down  to  their  cubs.  When  the attacks  in  the  Sundarbans  started getting  completely  out  of  control,  the locals  had  to  figure  out  a  way  to  defend themselves  without  just  wiping  out  the entire  tiger  population. The  tigers  would  stalk  the workers,  see  the  mask,  get  confused,  and walk  away.  But  eventually,  the  attacks started  happening  again.  The  tigers actually  figured  the  trick  out. The  tigers  adapted  their  hunting strategy,  bypassed  the  masks,  and resumed  killing  the  workers.  Turns  out  a $2  piece  of  plastic  from  a  party  store isn't  going  to  fool  an  apex  predator forever.  Who  would  have  thought?  You  can try  to  outsmart  a  man-eater,  but eventually,  they're  going  to  catch  on. But  this  raises  a  really  interesting point.  If  normal  tigers  usually  avoid humans,  why  do  some  of  them  suddenly cross  the  line  and  become  man-eaters  in the  first  place?  When  a  big  cat  decides to  start  hunting  humans,  it's  almost always  just  because  their  bodies  are failing  them.  To  hunt  a  deer  or  a  wild boar,  a  tiger  needs  to  be  in  peak condition. The  fourth and  final  animal  is  the  African  lion. Lions  fall  into  the  exact  same  trap  as tigers.
