NASA sent spiders into zero gravity aboard Skylab 3 to discover whether they could still build a spider web without the normal pull of gravity. The 1973 Skylab spider experiment followed Arabella and Anita, two orb-weaving spiders whose behavior and webs changed in microgravity. What began with a question from 17-year-old student Judith Miles became one of NASA’s most memorable animal-behavior experiments in space. NASA Skylab, spider behavior, space experiment.

At first, Arabella could barely move normally inside the enclosure. But within days, something appeared that gave NASA an answer, and raised an even stranger question about how spiders use gravity to organize their webs.

This video follows Judith Miles’ original idea, Arabella and Anita aboard Skylab 3, the webs they produced in orbit, and later research exploring what spiders use when gravity is no longer available as an orientation cue.

NASA sent bees into zero gravity aboard three Space Shuttle missions to learn whether they could fly, build honeycomb, and dig tunnels without a familiar sense of up and down. The 1982 Columbia experiment followed 14 worker honey bees struggling to control their flight, while a much larger colony aboard Challenger built honeycomb in 1984. What began with a question from high school student Todd Nelson became a story spanning two decades, ending with Liechtenstein students’ carpenter bee experiment aboard Columbia in 2003. NASA Space Shuttle, bee behavior, space experiment.

At first, some of the bees tumbled and drifted inside a small experiment box. Two years later, thousands of honey bees built comb, and their queen laid eggs. The final experiment asked whether carpenter bees would dig different tunnels in space, but the planned examination after landing never happened.

This video follows Todd Nelson’s STS-3 flight experiment, Dan Poskevich’s STS-41C honey bee colony, and the Liechtenstein students’ STS-107 carpenter bee project. We remember Columbia’s seven crew members and distinguish what the experiments showed from the questions left unanswered.

Fruit flies lay their eggs in alcohol to protect their children from parasitic wasps. Garden ants were practicing social distancing long before 2020. And Florida carpenter ants spend at least forty minutes chewing off a sister's infected leg, and nine times out of ten, she survives.

In this video, we trace how science discovered that insects get sick and how their colonies fight back with social immunity: the 1996 fruit fly experiment that led to a Nobel Prize and to how we understand our own immune system, the barcoded ant colonies that rebuilt themselves around a deadly fungus, the sick ants that walk away to die alone, the colonies that destroy their own infected babies, the first vaccine ever approved for an insect, and the 2024 discovery of ant amputations.
