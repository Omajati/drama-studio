PROJECT CONTEXT:
Project: a 15-minute English YouTube nature documentary for adults, channel "Insects" of the Animals network, titled "The Wasp That Turns a Cockroach Into a Zombie". Story: the emerald cockroach wasp (jewel wasp, Ampulex compressa) stings an American cockroach twice, first in the body, then in the brain; the cockroach grooms, the wasp cuts its antennae short and leads it by one antenna like a dog on a leash into a burrow, lays one egg on its leg and seals the entrance; the larva develops inside and an adult wasp emerges weeks later. Second line: laboratory experiments (stinger with touch sensors, swimming test, a drug that restores walking) show the venom removes the cockroach's will to move, not its ability. Then cockroaches that fight back with kicks, a failed release in Hawaii, and tiny parasitic wasps that save cassava in Africa and tomatoes in greenhouses. Tropical kitchens and gardens at night and dusk, laboratories in daylight.

Visual style (used for most images): photorealistic macro nature photography, shallow depth of field, natural soft light, crisp detail. Diagrams and life cycles: vintage entomology plate illustration in ink and watercolor on cream paper.

Recurring characters (keep identical across shots):
WASP — the jewel wasp (slender metallic blue-green emerald wasp, rust-red thighs, exactly six legs, two curled antennae, four clear wings, shorter than a paperclip).
COCKROACH — the American cockroach (glossy reddish-brown, pale-edged plate behind the head, exactly six spiny legs, two long antennae, about twice the length of the wasp); after the wasp's bite its antennae are cut to half length.

Hard rules: 16:9, no text or letters or numbers in the image, no watermarks, no logos, no signs, no labels on diagrams, no recognisable real people (people only as hands, feet or silhouettes from behind). Anatomy in every insect shot: exactly six legs and two antennae; the wasp has four wings; spiders, if any, eight legs and no antennae. No blood, no crushed insects, no larvae inside a living body in close-up — the larva is shown small, from a distance or as a diagram. No large spider or scorpion anywhere in this project.
