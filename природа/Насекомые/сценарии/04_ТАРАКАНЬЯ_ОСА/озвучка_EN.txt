You are a cockroach. It's a warm night in a kitchen somewhere in the tropics, and you are doing what your family has done for more than three hundred million years. You're finding crumbs in the dark. Every hair on your body is listening for danger, and you are very, very good at running away.

Within the next hour, you will walk into a dark hole in the ground, on your own six legs. Nobody will drag you there. Something shining green, half your size, will hold you by one antenna, and you will follow it like a dog on a leash. You will not be dead. You will not be paralyzed. You could run at any moment. You just won't.

Three things are coming that you won't forget. A needle that can feel its way to a brain. An insect kept alive on purpose, for days, as fresh food. And the third is about you, because the chemical this tiny animal switches off in a cockroach has a cousin in your own head, the one that gets you off the couch.

Why would the best escape artist on the planet ever stop running? A cockroach is built to flee. At the back of its body sit two little prongs covered in hairs so sensitive they feel the puff of air in front of a swinging hand. The roach turns and sprints before your brain has even finished deciding to swat. For its size, it covers ground like a cheetah. You have chased one across a kitchen floor. You know.

So the animal in this story should be impossible to catch. And yet, across the warm parts of Africa, South Asia and the islands of the Pacific, there is a hunter that catches it again and again, and never once has to carry it home.

It's called the emerald cockroach wasp, or the jewel wasp. Scientists know it as Ampulex compressa. The female is shorter than a paperclip, with a body of metallic blue green that flashes like a beetle wing, and thighs the color of rust. She has six legs, two curled antennae and four clear wings. She doesn't sting people unless you really insist. Her whole life is built around one kind of victim, the big American cockroach, an insect often twice her length and much heavier.

You'll often hear that the wasp paralyzes the cockroach. Hold on to that word, because it's wrong, and the way it's wrong is the most interesting thing in this story.

How do you subdue an animal twice your size that can outrun your reflexes? You start by making sure it can't hit back. The wasp lands, grabs the cockroach by the plate behind its head, and pulls it close. Then she curls her abdomen forward and slips her stinger into the middle of its body, right into the bundle of nerves that runs the front legs.

That first dose is quick and blunt. For two or three minutes, the cockroach's front legs go limp. It can't kick her with them and it can't push her away. The wasp has bought herself a short, quiet window, and she does not waste it.

Picture holding a horse still with one hand, just long enough to whisper in its ear. That's what comes next.

What kind of stinger can find a brain it can't see? The wasp pushes her stinger up through the soft throat of the cockroach, into its head. This is not a random jab. She is searching, and she has the equipment to search with.

In twenty fourteen, a team at Ben Gurion University in Israel, working in the lab of a neuroscientist named Frederic Libersat, looked at the tip of that stinger under a microscope. It was covered in tiny sensory bumps. The stinger isn't just a needle. It's a fingertip. It feels the difference between soft brain tissue and everything around it.

The team tested it in a way that sounds almost cruel to the wasp. They gave her cockroaches whose brains had been removed, or replaced with a soft gel. The wasp kept probing, and probing, for many times longer than usual, like someone feeling around in a dark drawer for keys that aren't there. When the scientists removed the sensory bumps from the stinger itself, the search stretched from about a minute to nearly twenty minutes.

When she finds what she's looking for, she injects venom into two precise spots in the head. One is a region that helps start and keep up movement. The other sits deeper, in a part of the brain that helps decide what the body does next. The whole thing takes around a minute. Then she lets go.

What does a cockroach do in the first minutes after a wasp has been inside its head? It cleans itself. Its front legs come back to life, and it starts grooming, carefully and obsessively, wiping its antennae, polishing its legs, for something like twenty minutes to half an hour. Researchers in the same lab traced this to the venom itself. Something in it pushes the roach into a cleaning loop.

And the wasp walks away. She doesn't hold it, doesn't guard it, doesn't even watch. She goes off to find a hole, a crack under a stone, a gap in the soil. She's not worried, because she already knows the cockroach isn't going anywhere.

When she comes back, she does something strange. She bites off about half of each of the cockroach's antennae and drinks a little of the fluid that seeps out. It's a snack, and some researchers think it may also help her judge how much venom she delivered. Then she takes what's left of one antenna in her jaws, and pulls.

And the cockroach walks. Not dragged, not carried. It steps forward on six working legs, in a perfectly normal gait, and follows her across the ground. Libersat's team described it in plain words: like a dog on a leash. Scale it up to your size and it's a ten year old leading a horse by one ear, and the horse simply coming along.

So here's the question that runs through the rest of this story. If its legs work, and its body works, why doesn't the cockroach run?

What exactly did the wasp take away? This is where the scientists stopped thinking about paralysis. Because a paralyzed animal can't walk, and this one can walk beautifully. It just won't start on its own.

They tested it like detectives. Flip a stung cockroach onto its back, and it rights itself as quickly as a healthy one. Its muscles are fine. Its reflexes are fine. Then they put stung and unstung roaches in water. A healthy cockroach swims hard and keeps going, trying to escape. A stung cockroach swims too. But it gives up far sooner, and floats. Touch a stung roach in a way that would send a normal one running, and it barely reacts. Its body still has the ability. What's gone is the urge.

The venom doesn't cut the wires to the legs. It turns down the part of the brain that says, now, go.

And here's the part that makes neuroscientists sit up. In twenty oh seven, the same lab tried to give that urge back. Insects use a chemical called octopamine to help kick the body into action, a bit like a cousin of the adrenaline in you. When they injected a drug that mimics octopamine into the brains of stung cockroaches, the roaches started walking on their own again. Not completely. But enough to show that the wasp hadn't destroyed anything. She had turned a dial.

So the jewel wasp doesn't make a zombie by killing the mind. She makes one by stealing the wanting. Remember that. We'll come back to it, because it's the part about you.

Where does a cockroach go when it has no will of its own? Wherever it's led. The wasp walks it down into the hole she found, and the roach settles in the dark. She climbs onto it and lays a single egg, smaller than a grain of rice, glued to the cockroach's middle leg. One egg per cockroach. That's the entire investment.

Then she leaves the burrow and begins filling the entrance. Pebbles, bits of leaf, grains of dirt, carried one by one and packed into the opening. A healthy cockroach could push its way out of that barricade in seconds. This one sits behind it, in the dark, and waits.

Researchers found something even colder. The venom also seems to change the cockroach's metabolism, so its body burns through its reserves more slowly and stays alive longer in the dark. Obedience is only half the job. The other half works like a refrigerator. The meal stays fresh.

What's about to happen in that burrow is exactly the kind of thing that made Charles Darwin doubt the kindness of nature.

How do you eat an animal from the inside without killing it too early? About three days after the egg is laid, a tiny larva hatches on the cockroach's leg. For a few days it feeds from the outside, through a small opening it makes in the leg. Then it chews its way into the body and moves in.

What follows is careful. The larva eats the organs it doesn't need the cockroach to keep, and leaves the ones that keep it breathing for as long as possible. A body that's alive doesn't rot. And the larva has a second trick for that. In twenty thirteen, a team led by the German biologist Gudrun Herzner found that the larva coats the inside of its host with its own saliva, a mix of antibacterial compounds. It disinfects its room before it moves in. One of those compounds, micromolide, had been studied earlier as a possible lead against the bacteria that cause tuberculosis.

Eventually the larva spins a brown cocoon inside the hollow shell of the cockroach, and by then the cockroach is gone. Weeks later, an adult wasp pushes its way out of what's left, climbs through the pebbles, and flies off into the light, metallic green and brand new. Then it starts looking for a cockroach of its own.

Charles Darwin knew about wasps like this. In a letter to the American botanist Asa Gray in eighteen sixty, he wrote that he could not persuade himself that a kind and all powerful God would have designed these wasps to feed inside the living bodies of caterpillars. Nature, for Darwin, was not a story with a moral. It was a story with a strategy.

But is every cockroach doomed the moment a jewel wasp lands? Not even close. In twenty eighteen, the American biologist Kenneth Catania at Vanderbilt University filmed these fights with high speed cameras, and he caught something nobody had really paid attention to. The cockroaches fight back.

An alert adult cockroach raises itself up on its legs like a boxer on tiptoe, guards its throat, and kicks backwards with its spiny hind legs, aiming at the wasp's head. Catania compared it to a karate kick. Out of fifty five encounters his team watched, adult cockroaches that defended themselves escaped the wasp about six times in ten. Young cockroaches almost always lost.

So the zombie isn't the cockroach that's weak. It's the cockroach that wasn't paying attention. And that's a lesson that works on more than one species.

Here's a twist that sounds like a joke. In the early nineteen forties, people actually shipped this wasp to Hawaii to fight cockroaches. It didn't work. The jewel wasp is fussy, territorial and slow, one egg, one roach, and the cockroaches of Hawaii were in no danger.

Its cousins, though, are a different story. Parasitic wasps are among the most useful insects alive. In the nineteen seventies, a pest called the cassava mealybug arrived in Africa and began destroying cassava, a root crop that millions of families depend on to eat. The Swiss scientist Hans Herren led a search across South America for its natural enemy, and found a tiny wasp, smaller than a sesame seed, that lays its eggs inside the mealybug. Released across Africa in the nineteen eighties, it brought the pest under control. Herren won the World Food Prize in nineteen ninety five, and by the prize committee's estimate, the work saved around twenty million lives.

Today, other tiny parasitic wasps protect tomatoes in greenhouses and corn in open fields. If you've eaten a salad this year, there's a decent chance a wasp you will never see helped grow it.

Which brings us back to you, the cockroach, in a warm kitchen at night. Why didn't you run? Not because your legs stopped working. Your legs were fine. A wasp shorter than a paperclip reached into your head and turned down the one chemical signal that says, get up and go. Everything else stayed. Only the wanting went.

And somewhere in your own brain, right now, chemicals with a similar job decide whether you'll get up from wherever you're sitting. You probably never thought of motivation as something that could be stolen. The jewel wasp has been doing it for millions of years.

You don't have to like cockroaches. You don't even have to like wasps. But next time you can't find the will to get off the couch, at least you'll know nobody stung you.
