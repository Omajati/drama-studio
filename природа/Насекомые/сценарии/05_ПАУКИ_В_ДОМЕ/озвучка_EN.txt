Right now, within a few steps of where you're sitting, there is almost certainly a spider. Scientists searched fifty homes room by room and found cobweb spiders in every one. And almost every spider on Earth is venomous. So which ones in your house can actually hurt you?

Is the most venomous spider on Earth really hanging over your bath? Look up at the corner above your bath. That loose tangle of threads, with a pale spider hanging upside down on legs as thin as hair, belongs to the cellar spider, the one many people call daddy longlegs. Its body is smaller than a grain of rice.

You've probably heard the legend. The daddy longlegs has the most powerful venom in the world, but its fangs are too short to pierce human skin. Both halves are wrong. In two thousand four, the television show MythBusters put it to the test, and Adam Savage let a cellar spider bite him. The fangs did get through. He felt a faint burning that faded within seconds. That was the whole story.

And here's the part nobody tells you. The cellar spider hunts other spiders, including ones far more dangerous than itself. It throws silk from a distance and wraps its victim before the victim can reach it. Your bathroom ceiling has a bodyguard. Danger to you: nothing worth counting.

What kind of spider turned up in every single house in that study? A cobweb spider, and very often this one: small, brown, round as a pea, sitting in a messy web in a window frame, behind the sofa, in the corner of the garage. The common house spider. The uncomfortable part: it belongs to the same family as the black widow. Same round body, same untidy web, same trick of flinging sticky silk over its prey with its back legs.

Same family doesn't mean same danger. Its venom is made for flies and ants. Press one against your skin and it might bite, and you'll get something like a mosquito bite, gone in a day or two.

Those fifty homes held more than five hundred kinds of insects, spiders and their relatives, and in two thousand sixteen the North Carolina State team's verdict was blunt: nearly all harmless, plenty helpful. This spider is one of the helpers. Danger to you: about the same as a mosquito.

What runs across your living room carpet on an October night, as wide as a child's palm? In Britain, across much of Europe and in the Pacific Northwest of America, it's very often the giant house spider. In autumn, adult males leave their funnel webs to look for females. That's why they suddenly appear in baths and across floors, fast enough to put grown adults on chairs.

Its close cousin, the hobo spider, has a far worse reputation in the United States. For decades it was blamed for rotting wounds, largely because of one experiment in the late nineteen eighties, when a researcher named Darwin Vest made hobo spiders bite rabbits and reported damaged skin. Later attempts to repeat it failed. In two thousand eleven, a team at Washington State University tested the venom and found no sign that it breaks down blood cells. And in Europe, where the hobo spider comes from, nobody ever treated it as dangerous.

Bites from both are rare and mild. And if one is sitting in your bath, it didn't climb up the drain. It fell in and can't grip the slippery walls. A glass and a sheet of paper will carry it outside. Danger to you: a nasty surprise, nothing more.

Who is most likely to bite you in your own bed? In much of North America and Australia, a strong candidate is the yellow sac spider. It's pale, almost see-through, smaller than your fingernail. It doesn't catch anything with a web. It hunts at night, walking across walls and ceilings, and spends the day in a little silk pouch where the wall meets the ceiling.

Because it wanders, it sometimes ends up in sheets and clothes, and when it's squeezed against skin, it bites. Medical books once blamed it for rotting wounds too. Then, in two thousand six, a team including the spider expert Richard Vetter gathered every case where the spider had actually been caught and identified. Twenty verified bites in the United States and Australia. Not one turned into a rotting wound. People got a sharp pinch, redness, some swelling, and a day or two of annoyance.

While we're in bed, one myth can go. You've heard that you swallow a handful of spiders in your sleep every year. There's no study behind it. To a spider, a sleeping human is a warm, breathing, vibrating mountain, exactly the kind of thing it avoids. Danger to you: roughly a bee sting.

Why do British newspapers print panic headlines about one small spider every autumn? Meet the noble false widow. Dark brown, round and glossy, about the size of your fingernail. It has been hiding in England since eighteen seventy nine, a stowaway from Madeira and the Canaries, and only in the last few decades did it break out across Britain and into Ireland, often living around windows and doors.

It's a cousin of the true widows, and that's where the headlines come from. Researchers at the University of Galway in Ireland spent years confirming real bites, with the spider caught and identified. In two thousand twenty two, they published a set of cases. Most bites hurt and passed within a few days. A few were genuinely rough: strong pain, shaking, nausea, and sometimes an infected wound that needed hospital care. Almost every bite happened with the spider trapped against skin, in bed or inside clothing.

No one is known to have died from a false widow bite. But it's a good reason to shake out the jacket that's been hanging by the back door all summer. Danger to you: real pain, rarely more.

How many venomous spiders can live in your house before somebody gets bitten? In two thousand one, a family in Lenexa, Kansas, found out. Their old stone farmhouse was full of brown recluse spiders. Over six months, they collected two thousand and fifty five of them and sent them to Richard Vetter. The family of four had lived there for years. Nobody had ever been bitten.

The brown recluse lives across the central and southern United States. It's tan, about the size of a large coin with its legs out, with a dark violin shape on its back. It's shy, hides in boxes and in clothes left on the floor, and bites mostly when it's pressed against skin.

Most bites stay small and heal on their own. In a minority, the venom kills a patch of skin, leaving an open wound that can take weeks to heal and sometimes a scar. Very rarely, mostly in young children, it affects the whole body. Deaths are extremely rare.

And here's what that Kansas house really proved. If thousands of recluses produced zero bites, then places where recluses don't even live can't explain all the recluse bites diagnosed there. Many of those wounds were something else. Hold on to that, because it's coming back. Danger to you: usually small, sometimes serious, very rarely worse.

Which spider waits exactly where you sit down? In nineteen seventy two, the country singer Slim Newton released a song about a redback on the toilet seat, and it sold more than a hundred thousand copies. The joke worked because it was true. Redbacks love the dark space under outdoor toilet seats, in sheds, mailboxes and garden furniture.

The redback and its American relative, the black widow, are the same kind of spider: glossy black, round as a pea, the female marked with red. Their venom works on nerves. With a bad bite, the pain can spread through the body, with sweating, nausea and aching that can last for days. People describe it as some of the worst pain of their lives.

Now the number that matters. Researchers looked at every widow bite reported to American poison centers from two thousand to two thousand eight. More than twenty three thousand cases. Not a single death. In Australia, redback antivenom arrived in nineteen fifty six, and since then no death has been firmly pinned on a redback bite. Danger to you: severe pain, very rarely more.

Is there a spider that could be in your house with a body count nobody argues about? Yes, one. In and around Sydney, on the east coast of Australia, lives the Sydney funnel-web. It's glossy black, big enough to cover a matchbox, with fangs strong enough to go through a fingernail.

Most of the year it sits in a silk-lined burrow in the garden. But in summer and autumn, especially after rain, the males go looking for females. They wander into garages and houses, hide in shoes left by the door, and fall into swimming pools, where they can survive for hours, holding a bubble of air in the hairs on their bodies.

The male's venom has a strange target. Cats and dogs cope with it surprisingly well. Primates, including us, do not. It attacks the nerves and can turn a bite into a life-threatening emergency very quickly. Thirteen deaths have been recorded. The last was a two-year-old boy named James Cully, in January nineteen eighty.

That same year, a team led by Struan Sutherland at the Commonwealth Serum Laboratories finished something people had chased for decades: a funnel-web antivenom. In January nineteen eighty one, a man named Gordon Wheatley became the first patient treated with it. He was out of hospital in two days. Since then, no one has died from a funnel-web bite.

And this is where the ladder turns upside down. To make that antivenom, someone has to milk venom from living male funnel-webs, a steady supply every season. At the Australian Reptile Park, many of those spiders are brought in by ordinary people, who catch them at home in jars and drop them off. The most dangerous spider that could ever walk into your house now keeps people alive, because your neighbors were brave enough to put a jar over it. Danger to you: a true emergency, and the reason that emergency now has an answer.

Which brings us back to that red, sore bump you once blamed on a spider. In two thousand eleven, doctors at the University of California, Irvine, looked at one hundred and eighty two emergency room patients who came in saying a spider had bitten them. A spider bite was confirmed in only seven. Most of the others had a skin infection, often caused by bacteria, which needed completely different treatment. The spider gets the blame. The real culprit is usually too small to see.

If something really does bite you, keep it simple. Wash the spot with soap and water, use a cold pack, and if you can do it safely, take a photo of the spider. If the pain spreads, or you feel sick, sweaty or shaky, see a doctor. And if you're in eastern Australia and a big black spider bites you, call for emergency help straight away, without waiting to see how it feels.
