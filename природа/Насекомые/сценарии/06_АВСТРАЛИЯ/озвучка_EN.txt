Everyone knows the rule about Australia: if it has eight legs, it can kill you. Except no spider there has been proven to kill anyone in over forty years.

Ten bugs in Australia, ranked by how seriously the experts take them. It starts with a spider wider than your hand that they handle bare-handed, passes an ant with its own hospital program, and ends with something the size of a sesame seed that can change what you eat.

Keep one question with you the whole way. Is Australia really the most dangerous bug country on Earth? By the top of the list, you'll have the honest answer, and it comes with a twist about that rule.

The spider most likely to make an Australian scream is one of the least dangerous on this list. It's flat, hairy and fast, and its legs can stretch wider than your open hand. The huntsman lives under bark, behind curtains, and, famously, in cars. Drivers fold down the sun visor and find one sitting there, legs spread across the mirror, perfectly still.

Its body is only about as long as your thumbnail. Everything frightening about it is legs. A huntsman can bite, and one kind, the badge huntsman, can leave a person sweating and sick for a few hours. But no one has ever died from a huntsman bite, and given the choice, it runs.

In two thousand twenty five, the University of the Sunshine Coast published a piece calling huntsmen hidden and hairy heroes, because they spend their nights hunting cockroaches and other pests inside people's homes. Spider keepers often let them walk across an open palm. Rung one: terrifying to look at, almost harmless to touch.

What glows a ghostly blue green in the dark of the outback? Shine an ultraviolet lamp across the red sand at night, and scorpions light up like tiny neon signs. Nobody is completely sure why their armor does that, but collectors use it to find them by the dozen.

Australia's biggest burrowing scorpions grow about as long as your finger, with heavy claws and a curled tail. They look like they belong in a desert movie. And the sting hurts, like a hot needle that keeps burning for hours.

To find out what Australian scorpions really do to people, a team led by the toxicologist Geoffrey Isbister spent two years collecting stings reported to poison centers and emergency departments, counting only cases where the scorpion was seen. The result, published in two thousand three, was almost boring. Pain, sometimes swelling, and then recovery. The scorpions that kill people live in other parts of the world. Experts here still move them with long forceps, because a sting is no fun. Rung two.

A caterpillar can make a horse lose her foal. In two thousand four, horse breeders in New South Wales saw a wave of pregnant mares losing their foals, with damage nobody in Australia had seen before. The trail led to an unlikely suspect: the hairy caterpillar of the bag shelter moth.

These caterpillars travel in long lines, nose to tail, which is why people call them processionary caterpillars. Each one is about as long as your little finger and covered in barbed hairs, much thinner than one of yours. The hairs snap off, drift onto grass, and get swallowed by grazing horses. From the gut, tiny fragments can work their way to the unborn foal. In that first outbreak, in the Hunter Valley, vets traced roughly a third of the abortions they investigated to these caterpillars.

For you, the danger is smaller: an itchy rash, or sore, streaming eyes if hairs blow into them. But the researchers who collect these caterpillars treat them like hazardous material, with gloves, long sleeves and eye protection. Rung three is the one you never meant to touch.

Which Australian spider was blamed for eating holes in human flesh, and never once did it? The white tailed spider. It's slim and dark, about as long as your fingernail, with a pale spot at the tip of its body, and it wanders through houses at night hunting other spiders.

For years, it was blamed for ugly, spreading wounds. Then in two thousand three, Geoffrey Isbister and a colleague followed one hundred and thirty people bitten by a white tailed spider that had been caught in the act. The number of rotting wounds was zero. Most bites caused pain and a red mark, and in almost half, a sore red patch that lingered.

So whatever caused all those horror wounds, the white tailed spider didn't. Rung four is a reputation that a single careful study took apart. So far, the reputations have been worse than the bites. From rung five up, that starts to flip.

Which Australian spider gets mistaken for the deadliest one, and in one toddler nearly matched it? Its venom is surprisingly similar. The mouse spider is stocky, glossy and black, with big jaws, and in one species the male has a bright red head. Unlike most dangerous spiders, males often wander in daylight, especially after rain, looking for females. Its body is about the size of a large coin.

In two thousand four, doctors reviewed the known mouse spider bites. Most caused pain and little else. But one case stood out: a toddler, nineteen months old, became seriously ill with symptoms very like a funnel web bite. The child was given funnel web antivenom and seemed to respond.

Serious bites are rare, and no deaths are on record. Still, experts in eastern Australia treat any bite from a big black spider as a possible emergency until they know otherwise. Rung five is the lookalike.

Australia's most common serious spider bite has an antivenom that, in a big trial, barely beat a dummy injection. The spider is the redback, which you've already met on this channel. A pea sized black body, a red stripe down its back, and a habit of living right where your hands go, under outdoor furniture, in sheds, inside old shoes.

Its bite doesn't usually look like much. Then the pain grows, spreads, and can come with sweating that won't stop. For decades, the answer was antivenom. Then, in two thousand fourteen, doctors ran a trial with two hundred and twenty four bitten patients. All of them got painkillers. Half also got antivenom, and half got a dummy injection. The antivenom didn't make a significant difference to their pain. That result changed how many doctors think about this bite.

Nobody has been proven to die from a redback in decades. But it hurts long enough that people who work outdoors wear gloves when they reach into dark corners. Rung six.

In Tasmania, an ant has its own hospital program. It's called the jack jumper. Black, with orange jaws, about as long as your fingernail, and with big eyes that seem to track you. When something bothers its nest, it doesn't wait. It jumps toward you.

For most people, the sting is a sharp, burning hurt. But by one Tasmanian study, nearly three in every hundred people there have had a whole-body reaction to its sting, and for the most allergic, one sting can shut the body down. Before anyone fought back, four deaths in Tasmania were put down to jack jumper stings.

So in two thousand one, the Royal Hobart Hospital started something unusual. People with a severe allergy get tiny, controlled doses of jack jumper venom, again and again, for at least five years, until the body learns to stop overreacting. Since the program began, the state reports no more deaths. Rung seven is the ant that doctors fight with its own venom.

And the spider you probably expected at the very top only makes rung eight. The Sydney funnel web. You've met it on this channel, so just the newest chapter of its story.

In January two thousand twenty four, the Australian Reptile Park showed off the biggest male funnel web it had ever received. They named him Hercules. He measured nearly eight centimeters from foot to foot, about as wide as a credit card is long. He was found on the Central Coast, north of Sydney, and before he reached the park, someone had handed him in at a local hospital.

Male funnel webs carry the most dangerous spider venom in Australia, and keepers milk them with extreme care. So why isn't this spider at the top? Because of what happens to spiders like Hercules. Their venom goes into antivenom, and since that antivenom arrived in the early nineteen eighties, nobody has died from a funnel web bite. Rung eight is the most venomous spider here, and also the most beaten.

So what does actually kill Australians? A team at the University of Melbourne went through fourteen years of records, from two thousand to two thousand thirteen. Venomous animals killed sixty four people in that time. Snakes killed twenty seven. Bees and wasps killed twenty seven too. Spiders killed none.

Bees and wasps also sent more people to hospital than any other venomous creature in the country, about a third of all admissions for bites and stings. The deadliest bug in Australia is a honeybee, smaller than your thumbnail, and it isn't even Australian. Settlers shipped it in from Europe in the early eighteen hundreds.

Its venom isn't especially strong. The danger is allergy. For most people, a sting is a bad minute and a sore spot. For a small number, it sets off a reaction that can kill within an hour. Beekeepers wear suits and veils for good reason. Rung nine has the body count.

Which Australian bug now worries allergy doctors more than any spider? Before it feeds, it's about the size of a sesame seed. It has eight legs. And it doesn't need venom to change your life.

This is the paralysis tick, which lives along Australia's east coast, waiting on grass and leaves for something warm to brush past. Once it bites, it can feed for days, swelling to the size of a fingernail. Its saliva carries a toxin that can paralyze. In the first half of the twentieth century, at least twenty people in Australia died from tick paralysis, most of them small children. It still paralyzes thousands of dogs and cats.

That was the old danger. Yet even this century, ticks have still been linked to deaths in Australia. Close to twenty years ago, a Sydney allergist named Sheryl van Nunen noticed something strange. Some of her patients were suddenly reacting to red meat, hours after eating it, and nearly all of them had been bitten by ticks. A sugar in tick saliva can teach the immune system to treat the same sugar in beef, lamb and pork as an enemy. One bite, and a steak can become dangerous for years.

In June two thousand twenty two, a sixteen year old named Jeremy Webb went camping with friends on the New South Wales Central Coast. He ate beef sausages, became sick, and died. At first, his death was put down to asthma. In February two thousand twenty six, a coroner found that he had died from a severe allergic reaction to meat, triggered by tick bites. It was the first confirmed death of its kind in Australia. The coroner warned there could be more. Only one similar death had ever been recorded anywhere in the world.

The bee still has the bigger body count. But the tick is the one that can rewrite your body for years, and the one most people don't know to fear. Even the experts won't squeeze it. Their slogan is freeze it, don't squeeze it, because squeezing can push more saliva into the skin. If one bites you, ask a pharmacist or a doctor how to remove it. Rung ten is the smallest creature on the list.

Is Australia, then, the most dangerous bug country on Earth? On the honest numbers, no. Its most famous killers have been beaten by antivenom, made partly from spiders that ordinary people drop off at a reptile park. In a typical year, the bugs that kill Australians can be counted on one hand, and most are bees.

What's left is the small stuff. The ant in the grass, the bee in the clover, the tick on the back of your neck that you never felt. And remember the rule we started with? If it has eight legs, it can kill you. The rule was right about the eight legs. It just pointed at the wrong animal.
