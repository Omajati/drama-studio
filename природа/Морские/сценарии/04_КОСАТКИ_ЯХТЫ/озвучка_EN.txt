Why would a killer whale bother with a sailboat when the strait is full of food? Because this is not an ordinary killer whale family. The orcas of the Strait of Gibraltar are one of the smallest and strangest populations on the planet. There are only about forty of them. Fewer than fifty, by every count. And they are listed as critically endangered.

They live on one thing, mostly. Atlantic bluefin tuna. A big bluefin can be longer than a man is tall and heavier than a piano, and it can outswim almost anything in the sea for a short burst. So the orcas don't sprint. They chase. They run the tuna in long relays, sometimes for half an hour, until the fish is too tired to escape. Less a fight than a marathon that ends with dinner.

An adult female like Gladis is around six meters long. Park her next to a family minivan and she's a little longer, and she weighs several times more. Her dorsal fin is curved and about as tall as a kitchen counter. A grown male's fin goes straight up, as tall as a man.

And here's the first thing most people get wrong. Killer whales aren't really whales in the way people imagine. They're the largest dolphins on Earth. Big brains, family groups, and a habit of inventing new behavior and passing it on. The name itself is old sailor talk, and the story goes that Spanish whalers called them whale killers, because they'd seen them hunt whales. Somewhere along the way the words flipped around, and it's stuck to them ever since. Public relations was never their strong side.

Which is a little unfair, because the Iberian orcas don't hunt whales at all. They hunt tuna. And for decades they followed the tuna through the strait, next to fishing boats, ferries and cargo ships, without touching a single one.

Then, in twenty twenty, something changed.

What does it feel like when an animal heavier than your car decides your rudder is interesting? The first reports came in May of twenty twenty, from the Strait of Gibraltar. Sailors on small yachts described the same thing. A few orcas approach from behind, go straight for the stern, and start biting and shoving the rudder blade. The boat swings around. Sometimes it spins in a full circle. The whole thing lasts minutes, sometimes close to an hour.

By that summer, the reports were moving north along the coast of Portugal, and then up to Galicia in the northwest of Spain. That's not random. That's the route the tuna take, and the orcas follow the tuna.

Spanish and Portuguese researchers started matching the photos. Every orca has its own fin shape and its own pattern of nicks and scars, the way you have a fingerprint. And the same few animals kept showing up. Three of them in the earliest encounters. Two youngsters, and one adult female.

The researchers gave every orca they linked to the boats the same first name, Gladis. Then a second name to tell them apart. Gladis Negra. Gladis Gris. And the adult female, Gladis Blanca. White Gladis. It's a small population, so naming them all Gladis was practical. It also means that if you ever meet a Gladis out there, you already know what she wants.

Here's the strange part. The orcas almost never touch the hull, the sails, or the people. They go for one thing, the rudder. The one moving part under the boat that turns and pushes back when you grab it. And as soon as the boat stops and the rudder goes slack, they usually lose interest and swim off.

That was the first clue that this wasn't a normal attack. Predators don't leave because the prey stops moving. Players do.

But in twenty twenty, nobody was talking about play. They were talking about revenge.

Can a killer whale hold a grudge? That was the theory that went around the world. And it started with a real scientist, not a tabloid. Alfredo López Fernández, an orca researcher at the University of Aveiro in Portugal, suggested that White Gladis may have had a bad moment with a boat. Maybe a collision. Maybe she got caught in a line during illegal fishing for tuna. A moment of pain and panic, right next to a hull.

In his version, that moment changed how she saw boats. She started going after the part that hurt her, the part that moves. And the young orcas around her watched, and copied. That's how orcas learn almost everything. Hunting tuna in relays isn't instinct. It's taught, generation after generation, by watching the adults.

It's a powerful story. A wounded mother, a pod of students, a war on yachts. Newspapers loved it. Headlines called her the orca who trained an army. Online, people turned her into a hero of the oceans, taking revenge on rich people's boats.

And there's a real piece of evidence behind it. Gladis Blanca was in the very first encounters. Some of the youngsters involved later are her own family. Researchers have linked her siblings and her calves to boat contacts too.

But here's where the revenge story starts to fall apart. Nobody actually saw what happened to Gladis. There's no record of the injury, no date, no boat. It's a reasonable guess, made by a serious researcher, and that's all it is. A guess.

And there's a second problem. If this were revenge, you'd expect the angriest animal to do most of the damage. You'd expect the adults to lead, the whole family to join in, and the attacks to aim at people. That's not what the photographs show.

When researchers kept matching fins over the next few years, the picture shifted. The animals doing most of the ramming weren't grieving mothers. They were teenagers.

How do you go from three orcas to a crisis that Spanish maritime authorities have to issue warnings about? You let teenagers get involved. By twenty twenty-two, the encounters were happening in a long band from the Strait of Gibraltar all the way up to Galicia and into the Bay of Biscay. By twenty twenty-three, there were more than two hundred recorded interactions in a single year.

The researchers counted the animals involved. Out of a population of fewer than fifty, about fifteen orcas had been linked to the boats. Most of them young. Juveniles and teenagers, at the age when a killer whale has a body the size of a small truck and the judgment of a fourteen-year-old.

And now the damage got serious. A rudder isn't just a fin on the back of the boat. It sits on a post that goes through the bottom of the hull. Twist it hard enough, often enough, and the post cracks the hull around it. Water comes in through the floor.

In November twenty twenty-two, a sailing yacht went down off the coast of Portugal after the orcas worked on its rudder. In May twenty twenty-four, a yacht called Alboran Cognac sank in the Strait of Gibraltar. The two people on board were picked up by a passing tanker. In October twenty twenty-five, a French yacht went down off Peniche, in Portugal. In July of this year, a Swedish yacht sank off Galicia, and the two people on board were rescued. And just this month, a crew off Spain watched the orcas tear half their rudder away in eight minutes.

By the latest counts, that's more than seven hundred encounters since twenty twenty, and somewhere around nine boats lost, depending on who's counting. Over the same years, the number of people reported injured by the orcas themselves is zero. Hold onto that. We'll come back to it.

There's one more number that matters. After the peak, the encounters started dropping. Around two hundred in twenty twenty-three. Fewer the year after. And fewer again in twenty twenty-five, by the counts so far. They haven't stopped. But the craze looks like it's cooling.

And that word, craze, is exactly what some orca researchers had been saying all along. Because killer whales have done something like this before. Not with boats. With fish. On their heads.

What do you call a behavior that one orca invents, the whole neighborhood copies, and then everyone forgets? In nineteen eighty-seven, off the coast of Washington State, a female killer whale started swimming around with a dead salmon balanced on her head. Not eating it. Wearing it.

Within a few weeks, orcas in other families of the same population were doing it too. Salmon on the forehead, cruising along like it was the most normal thing in the world. Researchers had a name for it within the season. The salmon hat. Then, by the next year, it was gone. Nobody wore one anymore.

That's what a fad looks like in killer whales. Somebody starts something pointless and fun. The young ones copy it because the others are doing it. It spreads fast. And it dies out once it stops being new.

In twenty twenty-four, a photographer near Seattle caught an old male orca with a silver fish draped across his head, and the internet decided the salmon hat was back. Scientists were careful. One photo isn't a comeback. But it reminded everyone that orcas don't just learn useful things from each other. They also learn silly ones.

In twenty twenty-four, more than a dozen orca experts met to look at the boat encounters together. Their conclusion was blunt. They found no evidence that the Iberian orcas were being aggressive. What they saw looked like play. Young animals, a toy that moves when you push it, and a crowd of friends doing the same thing.

Another researcher, Bruno Díaz López, offered a twist on that. Maybe the boats are practice. A rudder moving through water behaves a bit like a tuna, fast and twisting. Young orcas who spend their lives chasing tuna might be sharpening the same moves on something that can't swim away.

And both ideas point at the same quiet cause. Food. Atlantic bluefin tuna were badly overfished, and then, under strict catch limits, they came back. More tuna means the orcas eat well. As Díaz López put it, if you eat well, you have more time to play.

So White Gladis may have started it. The pain theory could still be right about her. But what spread through the young orcas doesn't look like revenge. It looks like the hottest game in the strait.

So if a game can sink a yacht, why has nobody on those yachts been hurt by an orca? Think about what these animals are. A killer whale can take down a great white shark. Some populations hunt seals, sea lions, even young whales many times their size. An orca that can tip a seal off an ice floe could pull a person off a sailboat without breaking a sweat.

And yet, in more than seven hundred encounters, the orcas have gone for the rudder. Not the sailor leaning over the side with a flashlight.

It isn't just Spain. Across the whole world, there is no confirmed case of a wild killer whale killing a human. The closest thing anyone has on record happened in nineteen seventy-two, off the coast of California, when an orca bit a surfer named Hans Kretschmer. He needed many stitches. He lived. The usual explanation is that the orca mistook him for a seal and let go. That's the most serious bite on record, in all of history, from an animal that hunts sharks.

Captivity is a different story, and a darker one. Orcas kept in tanks have hurt and killed their trainers. But that's an animal under stress in a concrete box, not a free family in the open sea.

So why not us? Here's the second myth, and it's the most popular one online. That orcas spare humans because they're friendly, or because they somehow know we're intelligent too. Nobody has shown that. The better answer is more boring, and much stranger.

Killer whales live by food traditions. Each population eats what its mothers taught it to eat, and almost nothing else. Some only eat salmon. Some only eat seals. The Iberian orcas eat tuna. A human doesn't look like tuna, doesn't sound like tuna, doesn't move like tuna. To them, you're not on the menu, the same way a garden snail isn't on yours. You could technically eat it. It never crosses your mind.

And there's a second layer. Orcas are careful animals. They test new things gently before they commit. A rudder is a safe toy. It's hard, it moves, it doesn't fight back, and it doesn't bleed. A person is a big, unfamiliar mammal that splashes and yells. Every orca that joined in learned the game with the rudder. Not one of them learned a game with people.

So what did White Gladis actually start? Maybe pain. Maybe a bad night with a fishing line that nobody will ever be able to prove. But what spread through the strait wasn't a war. It was a game with rules. Go for the moving part. Leave when it stops moving. Never touch the people.

The rule is so consistent that it shaped the official advice. For years, Spanish authorities have told sailors roughly the same thing. If the orcas come, stop the boat if you safely can. Take your hands off the wheel. Don't give them anything to play with. The game only works if you play along.

And the orcas are still in danger themselves. There are fewer than fifty of them. Some sailors have reacted to the ramming with firecrackers, loud bangs and worse. Spain and Portugal protect these animals by law, and scientists keep testing gentler fixes, like rudders with rough surfaces that orcas don't enjoy touching.
