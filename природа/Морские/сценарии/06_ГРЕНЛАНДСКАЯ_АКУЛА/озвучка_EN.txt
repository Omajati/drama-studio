Nothing with a backbone is supposed to live four hundred years. Not tortoises. Not whales. Certainly not you. And yet, under the Arctic ice right now, there are sharks that may have been swimming since before Isaac Newton was born. They move slower than you walk. And most of them have a pale parasite hanging off each eye.

Stay to the end and you'll get a shark that turned up on a Caribbean coral reef, a dish that's poison until it's left to rot, and a heart as heavy as a ten-year-old child that had been beating for a century and a half.

This is the Greenland shark. And the question under this whole story is simple. Was the oldest one we know of really alive before Newton? And how does a body keep going that long?

If you'd rather understand the ocean than be scared of it, subscribe. Every story here takes one animal you thought you knew and turns it around.

There's no way to read a Greenland shark's age off its body. For most fish, you'd count growth rings in the ear stones or the bones, the way you'd count rings in a tree. The Greenland shark gives you nothing to count. Its skeleton is cartilage, about as hard as the tip of your nose, and it lays down no rings at all. For a century, scientists could only shrug.

Then a young Danish biologist named Julius Nielsen tried something nobody had done with a shark. He looked inside its eyes.

The core of an eye lens is built before birth and never replaced. The proteins at its very center are the same ones the animal had before it was born. So whatever carbon got locked in there on day one is still there, like a time capsule sealed the year the shark arrived.

Nielsen's team gathered twenty-eight female sharks, all caught by accident during fishing and survey work off Greenland, and radiocarbon-dated the centers of their lenses. They had one extra clock to help them. In the nineteen fifties and sixties, nuclear bomb tests flooded the air and the ocean with extra carbon, and anything born after that carries the mark. Only the smallest sharks in the sample had it, the ones under about two meters. Everything bigger had been born before the bombs.

From there, the team built a growth curve, and in August twenty sixteen they published it in the journal Science. The biggest female, about five meters long, longer than most family cars, came out at roughly three hundred and ninety-two years old.

Here's the part the headlines usually skip. That number comes with a margin of about a hundred and twenty years either way. Her real age could be as low as two hundred and seventy-two, or as high as five hundred and twelve. When a video calls her the five-hundred-year-old shark, that's the very top of the range, dressed up as a fact. Even the low end, though, makes the Greenland shark the longest-lived animal with a backbone that we know of so far.

So was she alive before Newton? Put her on a calendar, and it gets strange.

What was happening on Earth the year this shark was born? If the middle of the estimate is right, and she was caught a few years before that paper came out, she was born around sixteen twenty. That's the year the Mayflower crossed the Atlantic. Isaac Newton wouldn't arrive for more than twenty years, on Christmas Day, sixteen forty-two, by the English calendar of the time.

By the time Newton published his laws of motion, in sixteen eighty-seven, she'd have been in her sixties, and still, by her own species' standards, a child. Greenland sharks grow about a centimeter a year, roughly the width of your fingernail. The same study estimated that females aren't ready to breed until they're over four meters long, which works out to about a hundred and fifty years old. If she kept to that schedule, she became an adult around the time the American colonies declared independence. A teenager through the entire age of powdered wigs.

And she kept going. Through two world wars, the first steamships, and the bomb tests whose carbon would one day help date her, until a net brought her up in the twenty-first century.

Now the honest version. Push the margin to its low end, and she was born in the seventeen-forties, after Newton was already dead. Push it to the high end, and she's from around the year fifteen hundred, less than a decade after Columbus first reached the Americas.

So the Newton line is a maybe, and it should stay a maybe. The best single guess puts her birth before his. The full range doesn't promise it.

Which leaves a stranger question. How does anything last through all that, when it can barely be bothered to move?

How do you catch a seal when you swim slower than a person strolling through a park? In a study published in twenty twelve, a team led by the Japanese biologist Yuuki Watanabe fixed motion loggers to six Greenland sharks off Svalbard, in the Norwegian Arctic, and simply watched the numbers come in.

The sharks cruised at about a third of a meter per second. That's a little over one kilometer an hour. An ordinary walking pace is around five. You could keep up with a Greenland shark while checking your phone. Its tail swung once every seven seconds or so, slower than you breathe in your sleep. Even in their fastest bursts, the tags never caught one going faster than about three kilometers an hour. For a fish its size, it's the slowest anyone has measured.

The obvious guess is that an animal this slow must live on leftovers. And it does scavenge. Greenland sharks have been found with pieces of whale, moose, horse and reindeer inside them, and once a whole reindeer. Polar bear remains, too, though those bears were almost certainly dead before the shark arrived.

But here's where the lazy scavenger picture falls apart. A study of forty-five sharks from Svalbard found seal in more than a third of the stomachs that had anything in them, mostly ringed seals, along with plenty of cod, wolffish and haddock. Some of that prey was in such good shape it had clearly been caught alive. A ringed seal can outswim this shark without trying.

So how does it do it? The leading idea is that it takes seals while they sleep. Ringed seals often doze in the water, and a dark shape rising from below at walking pace makes almost no wake and no sound. Nobody has filmed it yet, so treat it as a hypothesis, the best one we have. It's a hunting style built on patience instead of speed. And patience, when you might live four centuries, is the one thing you never run short of.

Most Greenland sharks spend their whole lives with a living animal hanging off each eye. Get close to one, and there's a good chance you'll see a pale, ribbon-like creature dangling from the middle of its eye, swaying in the current like a tassel. It's a parasitic copepod, a tiny crustacean, a distant cousin of shrimp, roughly the length of a paper clip. It clamps onto the cornea with an anchor and spends the rest of its life there, grazing on the surface of the eye.

And it's everywhere. In one large survey off East Greenland, more than four in five carried one, usually on each eye, and almost none were clean.

Up close, it's grim. A study in the late nineteen-nineties found scarring and clouding on the cornea around each anchor point, and concluded the parasite could leave the shark badly impaired, possibly blind. That became the story. An ancient shark, blind, feeling its way through the dark by smell alone. You'll still hear it.

Then, at the start of twenty twenty-six, a team from Switzerland and California published the first proper look at the rest of the shark's visual system, in the journal Nature Communications. They found a retina built entirely from rods, the cells you rely on at night, packed tight and tuned to blue, the color of light that reaches deepest into the sea. And they found no obvious signs of age-related damage. In sharks thought to be well over a century old, the retina looked healthy. Even the parasitized corneas still let most of the light through.

So the parasite probably blurs the picture, and the eye behind it keeps working. Divers who've met these sharks have described them turning to follow the diver, which would be hard to do blind. As for the copepod, it has found about the most stable home in the ocean. As rental properties go, a four-hundred-year-old eyeball is hard to beat.

What keeps a heart beating for four hundred years? Part of the answer is the cold. Greenland sharks spend most of their lives in water only a degree or two above freezing, about as cold as a glass of water with ice cubes floating in it. Cold slows every chemical reaction in the body. Their metabolism runs at a crawl, which fits the slow swimming and the slow growth. And when you do everything slowly, you wear out slowly too.

The cold alone can't be the whole story, though. Plenty of fish live in icy water for a few decades and die. So scientists went looking in the genes.

In twenty twenty-four, a German team assembled the Greenland shark genome for the first time. It's roughly twice the size of yours, and more than two thirds of it is made of so-called jumping genes, stretches of DNA that copy themselves around the genome. Among the genes this shark carries extra copies of, the team found a network linked to DNA repair, the body's toolkit for fixing damage before it turns into disease. It's a lead, and the researchers were careful to call it that. Proving those genes are why the shark lives so long is work that hasn't been done yet.

Then there's the myth worth retiring. You'll read that the Greenland shark simply doesn't age. In twenty twenty-six, an Italian-led team examined Greenland shark hearts and reported in the journal Aging Cell that they show the same wear we find in old hearts everywhere: scarring, and a yellowish waste pigment that piles up inside aging cells. The heart ages. It just keeps working anyway. The researchers suggest low blood pressure and an unusually stretchy main artery may take the strain off, decade after decade.

So the shark ages like everything else. It simply does it on a schedule measured in centuries.

Why would anyone bury a shark under a pile of stones and wait weeks before eating it? Because fresh Greenland shark meat is toxic. To cope with the cold and the pressure of deep water, the shark packs its tissues with a compound called trimethylamine oxide, which helps keep its proteins in working shape. Eat the meat fresh, and your gut turns that compound into something that, by old Arctic accounts, can leave a person staggering as if drunk.

People in Iceland found a way around it centuries ago. The shark is gutted and left to ferment for weeks under gravel and stones, then cut into strips and hung up to dry for months. During the fermentation, the toxic compound breaks down. What's left is hákarl, pale cubes about the size of a dice, with a smell of ammonia strong enough to clear a room. It's still served in Iceland today, often with a shot of strong spirit to chase it. Visitors try it as a dare. Most of them try it exactly once.

These days, the danger runs the other way. Hardly anyone hunts Greenland sharks for food anymore, yet fishing boats still haul them up by accident, on longlines and in trawls set for halibut and shrimp, all across the North Atlantic. Julius Nielsen has said they end up as bycatch by the thousands. And the strangest part? Every one of the twenty-eight sharks his team dated came to them that same way, as bycatch, already pulled from the sea by accident. For an animal that may not breed until it's about a hundred and fifty, every adult lost is a century and a half of growing up, gone in a single haul. Conservation scientists now list the species as vulnerable.

And here's where it gets strange. Even the people who study these sharks keep getting surprised by where they turn up.

What is an Arctic shark doing on a Caribbean coral reef? In the spring of twenty twenty-two, a team led by Devanshi Kasana, a researcher at Florida International University, was lowering baited lines down the steep outer wall of Glover's Reef, an atoll off the coast of Belize. They were studying sharks along the reef. One of the lines came up with something none of them had ever seen there. A slow, grey, heavy shark with a blunt head and tiny eyes.

The team concluded it was a sleeper shark, most likely a Greenland shark, possibly a hybrid with its Pacific cousin. It was the first sleeper shark ever recorded in the western Caribbean.

It makes more sense than it sounds. Up top, Belize is turquoise water and white sand. A few hundred meters down, the sunlight is gone and the water is cold enough to feel like the North Atlantic. Tags have followed Greenland sharks deeper than fifteen hundred meters, nearly four Empire State Buildings stacked end to end. Which means they may be spread through the cold basement of the world's oceans far more widely than anyone thought, simply out of sight.

What does a heart look like after a century and a half of beating? In April twenty twenty-six, Ireland got to find out. A dead shark washed up on a beach in County Sligo, on the west coast of Ireland. It was first reported as a basking shark. It turned out to be the first Greenland shark ever recorded stranded in Ireland. A male, about three meters long, roughly the length of a sofa and a half. Scientists estimated him at around a hundred and fifty years old, born when Queen Victoria was on the throne, and he seemed to have only just reached adulthood.

They removed his heart. It weighed about thirty-four kilograms, as heavy as a ten-year-old child. It showed some of the scarring that comes with age, and by every sign it had been doing its job. Why he died is still unknown. His eye lenses are going to be dated, the same way Nielsen dated those twenty-eight females.

Was the oldest one alive before Newton, then? Here's the honest answer. The best estimate we have says probably, by about twenty years. The margin of error says it can't be sure. What's no longer in doubt is that Greenland sharks swimming today were born centuries ago. And how does a body last that long? Cold water, a slow metabolism, patience, and genes that may be unusually good at repairs. Even then, the heart still ages. It just takes its time.

So let's go back to the rule we started with. Nothing with a backbone is supposed to live four hundred years. Somewhere under the Arctic ice tonight, a grey shape is moving through the dark at walking pace, a pale parasite trailing from each eye. It was already old when your great-grandparents were born. It will very likely still be there long after you're gone.

It's in no hurry. It never has been.
