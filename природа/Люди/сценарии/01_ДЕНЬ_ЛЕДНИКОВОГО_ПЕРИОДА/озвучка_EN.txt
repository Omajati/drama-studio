What does an Ice Age morning look like, if you're not in a cave? Because almost nobody here lives in one. This is the first myth to go. The people of this valley camp in the open, on a slope above a river, under a ridge of limestone hills. Today we call the place Dolní Věstonice and Pavlov, in the south of the Czech Republic. Thirty thousand years ago it was a highway. Herds of mammoth, reindeer and horse funneled through this valley, and the people sat right where the animals had to pass.

And how do we know any of this, after thirty thousand years? Because the wind did us a favour. All through the Ice Age, cold dry winds blew fine yellow dust across this valley, a soil called loess. Layer by layer it buried the camp, the hearths, the bones and the broken tools, and sealed them like a time capsule. In the eighteen eighties a farmer's plough began to turn up mammoth bones, and archaeologists have been digging here for more than a hundred years since.

They found the outlines of huts, with a hearth in the middle of each one, rings of stones and bones holding down the edges, and mammoth bones by the ton. Charcoal from the hearths, and later the bones of the people themselves, were dated by radiocarbon. Most of the dates cluster between about thirty-one and twenty-nine thousand years ago. That part is fact.

Here's how we think the morning looked. Somebody blows the hearth back to life from last night's embers. Somebody else steps outside to check the sky. And everyone, before anything else, puts on their shoes.

How can we know that, when not a single shoe survived? Their toes told us. Erik Trinkaus, an anthropologist at Washington University in St. Louis, measured the small toe bones of people buried here. Toes that grip bare ground grow thick and strong. Toes that live inside a snug shoe grow thinner. In two thousand five he reported that three people from this site had the thin, lazy toes of shoe wearers. So the first thing you'd do on an Ice Age morning is exactly what you did today. Think about your Monday. Alarm, shoes, out the door. Theirs was fire, shoes, out the door. And if your feet were cold this morning, so were theirs. Theirs were just cold at minus twenty Celsius, about minus four Fahrenheit, on a winter night.

What did they eat for breakfast? If you picture a caveman tearing at a leg of meat, you're picturing the second myth. For decades, scientists assumed Ice Age Europeans lived almost entirely on meat, simply because bones survive and plants rot. Then someone looked at a rock.

In two thousand ten, a team led by Anna Revedin published a study in the journal Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences. They had examined a grinding stone from Pavlov Six, a small camp just a short walk from this one, with a hearth and the remains of two mammoths. Stuck in the tiny pits of the stone were grains of starch. Cattail roots. Ferns. Plants that had been pounded and ground about thirty thousand years ago. The same team found the same kind of evidence in Italy and in Russia.

So these people were making something very close to flour. That's the fact. What they did with it is reconstruction. The likeliest answer is a thick paste or a flat cake, cooked on a hot stone by the fire. Think of it as the oldest pancake in Europe. It probably tasted of smoke and swamp, but on a frozen morning it was warm, filling, and it didn't run away.

Here is why that matters for our question. Grinding roots takes time. Digging them out of a frozen marsh takes more. A group that only just survives doesn't usually spend its mornings on slow, fussy food. A group with a little room to breathe does. Hold on to that idea. It's going to come back.

And here's the bridge to you. When you eat your toast this morning, you're doing something these people already did. Turning grass and roots into bread is not a gift from the first farmers. It's at least thirty thousand years old.

So who went hunting, and what did they actually catch? Everyone expects the answer to be mammoth. The bones are everywhere here. Piles of them, from many dozens of animals. But here's where it gets contested. Did these people hunt full-grown mammoths, as big as an African elephant and covered in a coat of hair? Or did they mostly collect bones from animals that died in the valley on their own? One camp of researchers points to the sheer number of bones and the spear points. The other camp points out how dangerous a living mammoth is and how many animals die naturally at a river crossing. Today, most think they did some of both. Nobody can tell you how often.

What surprised everyone was the smaller stuff. At Pavlov, the bones of arctic foxes, hares and wolves turn up in huge numbers. Animals you don't hunt for meat. You hunt them for fur. And there is a clever way to catch a hare without running after it.

You use a net. And this is where the clay steps in as a witness. In the nineteen nineties, the archaeologists Olga Soffer, James Adovasio and Bohuslav Klíma took a close look at tiny scraps of fired clay from Pavlov. On their surfaces were impressions, pressed in while the clay was soft. Cords. Knots. Woven patterns, like the back of a basket or a mat. Dozens of them. These people were twisting plant fibre into string, and weaving it, more than twenty-five thousand years ago.

This part is a guess, but a good one. If you have nets, hunting becomes a group job. Children shout and drive the hares. Grandparents hold the edge of the net. A strong young hunter is useful, but not essential. In other words, the Ice Age workday may have looked less like a lone hero with a spear and more like a family pulling a net through the snow, laughing when the hare escaped anyway.

What would you do with your afternoon, if the work for the day was done? On this hillside, somebody did something no human had ever done before. They built a fire hot enough to change the nature of the earth itself.

Up the slope, away from the main camp, Bohuslav Klíma excavated a small hut in nineteen fifty-one. Inside it was a kind of oven, an enclosed hearth with walls made of clay and earth. Around it lay hundreds of small pieces of fired clay, and across the whole site, thousands. Animal heads. Bits of bodies. Little balls with fingerprints. This is the oldest ceramic workshop known anywhere. Pottery for cooking would not appear for another ten thousand years, and on the other side of the world.

Read that again in your head. These people invented ceramics, and they did not make a single pot. They made figures. Bears, lions, mammoths, wolves, and people. The most famous of them is a small woman made of fired clay, found in nineteen twenty-five. We call her the Venus of Dolní Věstonice.

And here's the strange part. A huge share of the clay figures are broken. Not smashed by a falling roof or a careless foot. Cracked apart in the fire. In nineteen eighty-nine, Pamela Vandiver and her colleagues published a study in the journal Science. They tested the local clay and found that it handles heat very well. It shouldn't explode that often, unless you put it into the fire while it's still wet. Their conclusion was that many of the figures may have been made to burst in the flames on purpose. A pop, a crack, a shower of sparks, while everyone watched.

That's the contested part. Some researchers call it a ritual. Others say it was simply beginners learning a new trick, and failing a lot. Both sides agree on one thing. You don't experiment with fire and clay, again and again, for fun or for magic, unless you have time to spare.

Could a child really leave a mark that lasts thirty thousand years? On this site, one did. And it is on the most famous object of all.

In two thousand four, researchers scanned the Venus of Dolní Věstonice in a medical tomograph. On her back they found a fingerprint, pressed into the clay before it was fired. The anthropologist Miroslav Králík and his colleagues measured the ridges. They came from a child, roughly between seven and fifteen years old. That's a fact. Whether the child made the figure, or just picked it up while it was still soft, nobody knows. This part is a guess, but think of how children behave around anything adults care about. They touch it.

And the Venus still had one more secret. In two thousand twenty-four, a Czech team led by Petr Neruda published a micro-CT scan of her in the Journal of Archaeological Science. For years, some writers had claimed she was made of clay mixed with crushed mammoth bone and ivory, a kind of special recipe. The scan found no bone and no ivory at all. Just local loess, the yellow wind-blown soil of this very hillside, with tiny fossils and grains of limestone in it. She was shaped from one single piece. Somebody picked up a handful of the ground they lived on and turned it into a person.

So here is the third myth. Ice Age childhood was not just a short, grim apprenticeship in survival. Children were in the workshop. They were near the fire while adults made things that had no practical use at all. If you've ever left a fingerprint on wet paint, or on a fresh cake, you already know exactly how this happened.

How far did news travel in a world without roads? Further than you think. The people here wore beads and pendants made from shells that came from far away, carried or traded across hundreds of kilometres, hundreds of miles. And just last month, a new study made that world look even smaller.

On the twenty-third of October, two thousand twenty-six, a team led by Pere Gelabert and Ron Pinhasi of the University of Vienna published their results in the journal Science Advances. They read the DNA of a woman who lived about thirty thousand years ago at Isturitz Cave, in the far south-west of France, near the Spanish border. They expected western ancestry, and she had it. But she also carried a connection to the people of central and eastern Europe, the same broad family as the hunters of this Moravian hillside. Two worlds that earlier studies had kept apart were, in her, mixing. The authors suggest Isturitz may have been a meeting place.

Here's where it gets contested. The study read the genomes of only three people. Some of its timing estimates are uncertain, and the authors say so themselves. So nobody is claiming that a girl from Moravia walked to the Pyrenees. What the study does show is that people, partners and ideas moved across a frozen continent, over many generations, far more than we used to think.

Here's the bridge to you. You probably have a friend or a relative who lives far away, and news from them arrives by phone. Theirs arrived by a stranger walking into camp. Same need. Much slower delivery.

What happens on an Ice Age evening, when the light goes at four in the afternoon? The hearth becomes the centre of the world. Somebody stirs it, the hides at the door get pulled shut, and the long dark begins.

Who else was sitting by that fire? There is one more contested guest. At Předmostí, a famous camp of the same culture about a hundred kilometres, some sixty miles, to the east, a Belgian team led by Mietje Germonpré described a skull of a large canid, with a piece of mammoth bone placed between its teeth after death. They called it one of the earliest dogs. Many other researchers disagree, and say it is simply a wolf, and that the bone may have got there by chance. Here's where the evidence stands today. No one has yet shown, with ancient DNA, that animals like this were the ancestors of the dogs we know. So the honest answer is maybe. Maybe a half-tame wolf lay near the hearth. Maybe it was just a hunted wolf. If you have a dog asleep at your feet right now, you're living the version that hasn't been proven yet.

Evenings are when the camp probably did its quiet work. And quiet work was the real survival skill here. A spear kills one animal. A well sewn coat keeps a child alive for a whole winter. The fit of the hood, the seams that keep out the wind, the boots our toe bones told us about, all of that was made by hand, by firelight. Sewing hides with bone tools. Twisting cord. Carving ivory. Telling the stories that held a group together. That part we can only reconstruct. But the evening is also when the people of this valley did something we can see very clearly. They took care of their dead.

In nineteen eighty-six, archaeologists here found three young people buried side by side in one grave. Their heads were covered in red ochre. They were laid out carefully, with ornaments of teeth and ivory. Another grave, found back in the late nineteen forties, held a woman buried under two huge mammoth shoulder blades, with red ochre around her. In the same camp, someone carved a tiny head from mammoth ivory, with a face that droops on one side. Some researchers have wondered whether the carving shows the woman in that grave, whose face may have been uneven too. That's a guess, and it's contested. But it would make it one of the first portraits of a real person.

People who bury their dead with this much care, and carve their faces, are not people who spend every minute fighting not to die. Which brings us to the answer.

So did they have free time? No one can clock an Ice Age workday, and anyone who gives you an exact number of hours is guessing. But look at what they left behind. Flour that took hours to grind. Nets that took days to knot. Hundreds of clay figures, made to be beautiful, or made to explode. Graves prepared with ochre and ornaments. Every one of those things costs time that survival alone does not need.

And here's the twist. Ceramics, the technology that would later give us pots, bricks, toilets and the cup you drink your coffee from, was not invented to be useful. On this hillside, the very first fired clay on Earth was made for figures. For animals and people. Maybe for fun, maybe for magic, maybe just to watch them burst in the fire. Usefulness came ten thousand years later. Imagination came first.
