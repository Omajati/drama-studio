# Why Do Wild Animals Follow Humans for Miles Without Attacking? · 564K просм. · 16.6 мин · Poko · 2026-09-28 · hhskzT5puRw
Теги: 
You're hiking alone, late afternoon, the trail winding up through the pines. You feel a prickle on the back of your neck. You turn around, and forty feet back, a cougar is standing on the path, watching you with two flat golden eyes. You step, it steps. You stop, it stops. For twenty minutes it shadows you down the mountain. Then, at the tree line, it melts into the brush and is gone.

It had every chance to attack. So why didn't it?

This happens more often than you'd think. Wolves shadow hikers, coyotes trail joggers, and bears amble behind campers for miles. We break down the stacked instincts behind it:

Fear. In UC Santa Cruz playback experiments, pumas fled recorded human voices and abandoned fresh kills, while ignoring frog calls. The 2019 follow-up showed that fear rippling down the food chain.
The math of the hunt. Lions catch gazelles only about a quarter of the time, wolves land roughly one elk in five, and a single injury can be a slow death sentence for the hunter.
Ambush logic. A predator you can see is a predator that has already abandoned its stalk.
Curiosity. Neophobia turns you into a walking puzzle it has to study.
The escort. Colorado State's Kevin Crooks explains how cougars trail hikers, especially mothers with cubs, using bluff charges instead of attacks.
Leftovers. A food-conditioned animal isn't hunting you, it's hunting your backpack. "A fed bear is a dead bear."
Rival, not meal. Forward-facing eyes read as "predator," so some animals tail you the way a fox trails a wolf.

We're also honest about the exceptions, including the Tsavo man-eaters of 1898, and what to actually do if something follows you: don't run, face it, make yourself big, and back away slowly.

The twist: this exact instinct, following the strange biped instead of fleeing it, may have turned wolves into dogs. Every dog began as a predator that chose to follow instead of fight.

# Arkansas Released 254 Bears Into Land Infested With Feral Hogs — No One Expected These Results · 111K просм. · 21.8 мин · Range Shift · 2026-11-06 · ZqeSv2MXWzI
Теги: 
In 1958 the Arkansas Game and Fish Commission started trucking black bears down from Minnesota and Manitoba and releasing them in the Ozark and Ouachita National Forests. 254 bears over ten years, and almost nobody was told. Arkansas had been down to about 25 bears in 1928. Today it has thousands, a record 765 were taken by hunters in 2023, and the state calls it the most successful large-carnivore reintroduction in the world.

Nobody in 1958 was thinking about wild hogs. Now feral hogs are in every county in Arkansas, the task force removed more than 12,000 in 2024, and the damage runs to about 41 million dollars a year. The one animal in those mountains big enough to put a grown hog on the ground is the bear the state smuggled in. And this summer, when Arkansas opened the door to warfarin hog bait, the rule it wrote bans the poison in every county where a bear has been harvested since 2023.

This video reconstructs the release from the published record and tells the bears-and-hogs story as the record stands: black bears are known to prey on feral hogs of all ages, the 2021 Smoky Mountains film shows it, and nobody has counted how much difference it makes in Arkansas. Scenes of the 1958 releases are built from the Commission's own accounts; the title describes Arkansas as it is today.

When a wildlife tracking collar switches to a fast, steady pulse, it means the hardware has not moved for at least six hours. In the deep timber of northern Maine during the middle of February, a stationary collar usually means one thing. The animal wearing it has frozen to death, starved, or been killed. A state biologist strapped on snowshoes and followed the signal into a dense stand of spruce and fir. He expected to find a carcass. He did not expect to find a crime scene that would turn decades of predator hierarchy completely upside down. 

The collar belonged to a healthy adult Canada lynx. In the deep snow of the northern forest, the lynx is not supposed to be the victim. It is the undisputed apex hunter of the winter woods. Yet, this cat had not simply died of exposure. It had been ambushed, overpowered, and killed violently in the snow. And the tracks leading away from the body did not belong to a bear, a wolf, or a pack of coyotes. They belonged to something far smaller. By the time the state’s multi-year tracking study concluded, biologists would find that this was not a bizarre accident. It was a pattern. Something in the Maine woods was systematically hunting a federally threatened apex predator, and the culprit was an animal that most people assume is harmless.
