PROJECT CONTEXT:
Project: a 20-minute English-language wildlife documentary for adults, channel of land animals, titled "Something Ate the Everglades' Raccoons. It Came From a Pet Store". Story: night road surveys in Everglades National Park showed raccoons, opossums, bobcats, rabbits and foxes almost vanished; radio-collared marsh rabbits proved invasive Burmese pythons were eating them. The pythons came from the pet trade (the Hurricane Andrew story is only one theory); record snakes of nineteen feet and two hundred and fifteen pounds; they eat deer and alligators; searchers almost never see them. Scientists use radio-tagged male "scout" pythons to find big breeding females; the state pays hunters and runs the yearly Python Challenge. Side effects: mosquitoes now bite cotton rats that carry Everglades virus. Risk to people is very low; the snakes spread to the Keys and north toward Lake Okeechobee; a 2010 freeze may have made them more cold-tolerant. Nobody knows how many there are. Ending teaser: dangerous animals of Australia (cassowary, dingo, saltwater crocodile).

Visual style (used for every image): cinematic wildlife documentary photograph, natural light, earthy muted palette with one warm accent (headlights, a headlamp, golden hour). Pet-trade and 1990s flashbacks look like archival faded color photographs. Comparisons, maps and diagrams are naturalist engraving-style illustrations on warm parchment with one human silhouette for scale and no labels.

Recurring characters (keep identical across shots):
PYTHON — a very large female Burmese python, tan-olive skin with dark chocolate-brown blotches outlined in black, a dark arrowhead mark on top of the head, thick as a man's thigh.
RABBIT — a small dark-brown marsh rabbit with short rounded ears and a thin black radio collar with a short antenna.

Hard rules: 16:9, no text or letters or numbers in the image, no watermarks, no logos, no signs, no flags. No blood, no wounds, no bodies, no animals fighting, killing or swallowing prey in close-up: predation is shown only before or after (a still snake with a bulge in its middle, an empty road, an empty burrow). Animals with true-to-species anatomy, never cartoonish; snakes have no legs and no ears. Hunters, researchers, rangers and tourists are generic people, never recognisable real persons; they are seen from behind, in silhouette or with faces out of frame. Papers, screens, maps, tape measures and labels are always blurred and unreadable.
