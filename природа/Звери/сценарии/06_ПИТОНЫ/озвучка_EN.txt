The calmest night drive in America is a crime scene. On the main road through the southern Everglades, nothing crosses your headlights anymore. No raccoons. No opossums. No rabbits. Something ate almost all of them, and it came here from a pet store.

So here's the real question: how does an animal longer than a car disappear in a swamp next to millions of people? And how close has it already come to you?

Three things are waiting further down this road. A rabbit sent into the swamp as a spy. A snake that betrays its own kind. And a mosquito that changed its diet.

Ninety-nine out of every hundred raccoons. That's how much of the roadside raccoon count disappeared from Everglades National Park. In two thousand twelve, a team led by the biologist Michael Dorcas,, published the numbers. They had driven the park's roads at night, year after year, counting every animal caught in their headlights. Then they compared those counts with similar night surveys from the nineteen nineties, on the same roads, before anything strange had moved in.

Picture the older surveys first. A warm night, windows down, the truck rolling slowly past the sawgrass. Raccoons waddling across the asphalt, opossums frozen in the beams, a marsh rabbit bolting into the reeds. Nobody thought of any of them as rare. They were the background noise of the swamp.

And then the new counts came in. Raccoon sightings had fallen by more than ninety-nine percent. Opossums, almost ninety-nine percent. Bobcats, a predator that should be the hunter in this story, down by about seven eighths. And rabbits and foxes, two animals that used to be everywhere, were not seen at all. In tens of thousands of miles of driving, not a single one.

That kind of drop usually has a short list of suspects. A disease. Poison. Hunting. Roadkill. None of them fit. The losses were worst deep in the south of the park, where people barely go, and milder toward the edges. The pattern matched only one thing on the map: where Burmese pythons had been living the longest.

But a pattern on a map isn't proof. Dorcas and his colleagues said it themselves: the declines coincided with the snakes. To convict a predator, somebody had to catch it in the act. And in the Everglades, nobody ever catches a python in the act.

How do you prove a snake ate an animal that nobody ever sees die? A wildlife ecologist named Robert McCleery, from the University of Florida, came up with a brilliant, slightly cruel answer. If the rabbits were gone, he would bring rabbits back. And this time, every one of them would carry a radio.

His team caught marsh rabbits in places where they were still common, fitted them with small radio collars, and released them into the park, into the areas where rabbits had vanished. Other collared rabbits went to sites outside the park, where pythons were rare or absent. Then the researchers did the most boring part of science. They listened, every day, waiting for signals to stop moving.

Inside the park, the signals started going still. And when the team followed them through the wet grass, again and again they ended at a long, heavy shape stretched out in the vegetation, with a radio collar still sending its signal from somewhere inside it.

When the study came out in two thousand fifteen, the numbers were hard to argue with. Within eleven months, pythons accounted for about three quarters of the rabbit deaths inside the park. At the control sites outside, pythons killed none. Out there, the rabbits died the ordinary way, mostly to other predators, like bobcats. And by the end, the rabbits released into the park had essentially been wiped out.

That's the moment the case closed. For the rabbits at least, the snakes had been caught in the act, by radio. Which leaves the obvious next question. What is an animal from Southeast Asia doing in the middle of Florida at all?

A baby Burmese python fits in your cupped hands. That's how most of them came to Florida. Picture a pet shop in the nineteen nineties: a glass tank under a warm lamp, a hatchling about as long as your arm coiled on the bark, and a kid tapping the glass while a parent asks how big it gets. Calm, pretty and cheap, they were one of the most popular big snakes in the American pet trade. Tens of thousands were shipped in from Southeast Asia, and South Florida was one of the main doors they came through.

The trouble is what happens next. A python that fits in a shoebox at Christmas can be longer than its owner within a few years, eat rabbits instead of mice, and need a room of its own. Some owners found zoos or other keepers. Some opened a door near the edge of the swamp and drove away. And South Florida, for a snake from the wetlands of Southeast Asia, turned out to feel a lot like home.

There's a famous shortcut version of this story: in nineteen ninety-two, Hurricane Andrew wrecked a reptile breeding facility near Miami, and the snakes poured into the swamp. The storm may well have added some. But pythons were already being found in the Everglades years before Andrew. Nobody can point to the first snake. Most researchers think the wild population grew out of many escapes and releases over many years.

By two thousand, scientists confirmed that the pythons in the park were breeding. In two thousand twelve, the United States banned importing them into the country. By then, the snakes in the Everglades didn't need any more help.

And how big can one of these escaped pets get? In July two thousand twenty-three, a twenty-two-year-old hunter named Jake Waleri was out at night in Big Cypress National Preserve, right next door to the park, when he spotted a python on the road. He and his friends wrestled it off the road, and when the scientists measured it, it was nineteen feet long. That's as long as a giraffe is tall. It weighed about a hundred and twenty-five pounds, and it became the longest Burmese python ever recorded in Florida.

A year earlier, a different team had pulled out a female that was shorter but much heavier: almost eighteen feet and two hundred and fifteen pounds, the weight of a big man. Inside her were one hundred and twenty-two developing eggs. One snake, one season, enough eggs to fill a classroom with baby pythons.

The internet loves twenty-foot pythons swallowing everything in Florida. The real record is nineteen, and most of the snakes hunters bring in are far smaller. But the menu part is no exaggeration. Florida pythons have been found with raccoons, rabbits, wading birds, and even alligators inside them. In a report published in two thousand twenty-four, researchers described a python under fifteen feet long that swallowed a white-tailed deer weighing about two thirds as much as the snake itself. That's like you eating a whole grown teenager in one sitting, without hands, and then skipping dinner for weeks.

So why can't an entire state find a snake longer than a car? Stand at the edge of the Everglades and look out. Sawgrass to the horizon, cut by water, tree islands, mangroves. Now picture a mottled brown and tan pattern lying in that grass, completely still, maybe for days, because a snake that just ate a deer doesn't need to move. Your eyes slide right over it.

In two thousand twenty, a team led by Melia Nafus, working with the United States Geological Survey, put a number on it. The chance that a searcher spots a python that's really there is most likely below five percent. Even in places where a radio-tagged snake was known to be hiding, their best estimates of spotting it ranged from about one in ten thousand to under two in a hundred.

Think about what that means on a walk. A biologist with an antenna, a beeping receiver, the signal saying the snake is right here, practically under her boots. She looks, and looks, and sees grass. Somewhere among those stems, a python that weighs as much as she does is coiled up, waiting for her to leave.

That's why counting them is nearly impossible, and why catching them by simply looking is like trying to empty a lake with a teaspoon. Which is why the best python hunters in Florida eventually stopped relying on human eyes at all.

The best python hunter in Florida is a python. Out of Naples, on the Gulf Coast, a team from the Conservancy of Southwest Florida, led by the biologist Ian Bartoszek, has been running the same trick since two thousand thirteen. They catch a male python, implant a small radio transmitter, and let him go. They call these males scouts. Some people call them Judas snakes.

Here's why it works. In the cool months of breeding season, male pythons go looking for females, and they're very good at it. They follow scent trails through miles of swamp that no human could ever search. And the males are drawn to the biggest females around, the ones carrying the most eggs. So the team waits until breeding season, follows the beeping signal into the brush, and where the scout has stopped, there is often a female, sometimes with several other males wrapped around her.

Remember the two-hundred-and-fifteen-pound female with a hundred and twenty-two eggs? A scout found her. His name was Dionysus, Dion for short, and he led the team to her and to several other big breeding females over the years. One radio transmitter, one male python, and an entire future generation of snakes never hatched.

It's slow work. Every scout has to be tracked for months. But it's targeted at exactly the snakes that matter most, the big egg-laying females. In two thousand twenty-five, the Conservancy announced that, since it started, its program had removed more than twenty tons of pythons from its patch of Southwest Florida. Twenty tons of snake, found mostly by other snakes.

Florida pays people to hunt pythons by the foot. Since two thousand seventeen, the state's Python Elimination Program has paid contractors an hourly wage plus a bonus that grows with every foot of snake, a few hundred dollars for a record one. Picture one of their nights. A truck crawls along a gravel levee at walking speed, spotlights sweeping the canal. Hours of nothing. Then, in the beam, a dark band lies across the gravel from edge to edge. The hunter is out of the door before the truck stops, grabbing for the tail before the head reaches the water.

Then, once a year, the state turns it into a sport. The Florida Python Challenge is a ten-day competition open to almost anyone who takes a short online training. In two thousand twenty-six, more than nine hundred people from as far away as Vietnam signed up, and together they removed two hundred and eighty pythons, ninety-six of them caught by one man.

And the bigger picture sounds even more impressive. Between the contractors, the scientists, the Challenge and ordinary Floridians, tens of thousands of Burmese pythons have been pulled out of South Florida since two thousand. That's the good news.

Here's the uncomfortable part. Nobody can say whether all that hunting is actually shrinking the population. Every snake removed is a real snake that will never lay eggs again. But you can only measure how much of a problem you've solved if you know how big it was to begin with. And as we've seen, this is a snake you can stand next to and miss.

The strangest consequence of all this weighs less than a grain of rice and drinks blood. In the Everglades lives a mosquito called Culex cedecei, and it matters for one reason. It's the only mosquito known to spread Everglades virus, an infection that circulates mostly in small rodents and can sometimes make people sick.

In two thousand seventeen, a team from the University of Florida, led by the entomologist Nathan Burkett-Cadena, with his graduate student Isaiah Hoyer, checked what these mosquitoes were feeding on. They caught them at a site where scientists had done the same check back in nineteen seventy-nine, before the pythons. In nineteen seventy-nine, only about fifteen percent of the meals came from one animal, the hispid cotton rat. By two thousand sixteen, it was about three quarters.

The reason is simple and a little eerie. The mosquito used to spread its bites across a whole crowd of warm bodies: raccoons, opossums, deer, rabbits. The pythons removed most of that crowd. What's left in the grass are lots of small, fast-breeding cotton rats, and cotton rats happen to be one of the main animals that carry Everglades virus. A follow-up study in two thousand twenty-one found that in areas with pythons, more of the mosquitoes' meals came from these rats, and warned that this could raise the amount of virus in the mosquitoes.

To be clear, human cases of Everglades virus are rare, and nobody is saying there's an outbreak. But think about how this chain works. A snake that you will never see changed what the swamp's mosquitoes bite, which could change what's in the next mosquito that bites you.

Would a wild python in Florida ever go after you? It's the first thing people ask, and the answer is better than the movies suggest. In two thousand fourteen, two of the scientists who know these snakes best, Robert Reed of the United States Geological Survey and Skip Snow of the National Park Service, went through every known run-in between people and Burmese pythons in Everglades National Park over ten years.

They found five. Every single one involved a biologist wading through flooded marsh, doing research, often in murky water up to the knees. Two of the strikes left minor marks from the teeth. None involved the snake coiling around anyone. The authors thought the snakes were most likely making a feeding mistake, reacting to movement in the water, and then letting go. Their conclusion was that the risk of a person being killed by a python in the park appears to be extremely low.

Headlines have claimed that Florida's pythons are hunting people. Here's the real record. Across the United States, giant constrictors have killed people over the decades, and those were pet snakes inside homes. In the wild in Florida, despite all those snakes and all those visitors, there is no recorded death from a python. The real danger of this snake is to everything that's roughly raccoon-sized.

But close? They've been very close. In two thousand seven, researchers tracking an endangered Key Largo woodrat in the Florida Keys followed its radio signal and found it coming from inside a python. It was one of the first signs the snakes had reached the Keys. In South Florida, pythons have turned up in suburban neighborhoods and industrial areas, and by two thousand twenty-five, biologists considered them established as far north as Lake Okeechobee and the suburbs around Fort Myers.

For a while, people hoped the weather would stop them. In January two thousand ten, a brutal cold snap hit South Florida, and in some areas researchers documented that most of the pythons they were watching died. Then, in two thousand eighteen, a team led by the geneticist Daren Card compared the DNA of pythons from before and after the freeze. In the survivors, they found signs of rapid selection in genes linked to body temperature, behavior and metabolism. The cold snap that was supposed to end the invasion may have helped breed snakes that handle cold a little better.

How many pythons are hiding in Florida right now? The number that gets repeated is hundreds of thousands. It might be right. It might be wrong in either direction. Nobody knows, and the scientists say so openly. Park scientists say the best estimates so far point to at least tens of thousands. And in every breeding season, a single big female can lay dozens of eggs.

Remember the question we started with? How does an animal longer than a car disappear in a swamp next to millions of people? All it needs is stillness, a pattern that matches the grass, a place too big to search, and a life where one big meal can last for weeks. And how close has it come to you? Close enough to sit at the edge of the suburbs, close enough to reach the Keys. And it has reached somewhere closer still: the food chain — the raccoon that no longer crosses the road, the rabbit that never comes back, the mosquito that bites a rat instead. It reached you the long way round, through everything that used to live around you.

Night again, on the main road through the southern Everglades. The truck rolls slowly, windows down, headlights sweeping across the sawgrass. Nothing crosses. No raccoon waddling over the asphalt. No opossum frozen in the beam. Out in the dark, a scout python is crawling toward a female, a radio transmitter pulsing inside him, and somewhere ahead, a biologist is waiting for the signal to stop.

So if you ever drive that road at night and nothing crosses in your headlights, now you know why. Something got there first.
