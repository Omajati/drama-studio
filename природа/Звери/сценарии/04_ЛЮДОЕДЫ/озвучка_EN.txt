You are lying on a thin mat in a canvas tent, and the night outside is so dark you can't see your own hand. It's eighteen ninety-eight. You are in East Africa, and you came here to build a railway. Around the camp there's a wall of thorn bushes taller than a man. A fire is burning by the gate. Thirty other workers are asleep within shouting distance. You are, by every rule you know, safe. Then the canvas beside your head moves. Very slowly. As if something big is leaning on it, listening for your breathing.

For nine months, that sound meant someone in the camp would be gone by morning. Two lions did that. Two animals stopped the construction of a British railway, sent hundreds of workers fleeing on a train, and got themselves discussed by the Prime Minister in Parliament. And here's the part that should bother you more: a lion hunting people in eighteen ninety-eight is history. But in one forest on the other side of the Indian Ocean, a tiger can still decide that a human being is dinner. Every year. Right now.

Three things in this story sound made up, and none of them are. A man who wore his face on the back of his head. A museum drawer where a few hairs, stuck in a broken tooth, waited more than a century to tell us what those lions really ate. And a number of victims that everyone repeated for a hundred years, and that turned out to be wrong.

So keep one question in mind, because we'll answer it at the end. Almost every big predator on Earth avoids us. What does it take for one of them to look at you and see food?

Why would two lions stop an empire? Because nobody, at first, believed they were the problem. The place was called Tsavo, a dry scrubland in what is now Kenya, cut by a river of the same name. The British were building the Uganda Railway, a line from the coast at Mombasa to Lake Victoria. Most of the workers had been brought from India, thousands of them, living in tent camps strung along the track. In March of eighteen ninety-eight, a young engineer named John Henry Patterson arrived to build a permanent bridge over the Tsavo River.

Within weeks, men started vanishing at night. At first the camp had a simpler explanation. People run away from hard jobs. People get sick and wander off. Maybe someone was settling an old argument with a knife. Patterson himself didn't take the lion stories seriously, right up to the night a lion pushed into a tent, took one of his own foremen, and left the man's tentmate untouched. After that, nobody in Tsavo could pretend.

Here is what made it terrifying. These were not starving cats grabbing whoever stumbled into the bush. They came into the camps. They chose a tent, and they chose a man. Workers described them as patient and almost silent. Then they moved off to feed somewhere close enough that the whole camp could hear it. Lions are the only big cats that routinely hunt in teams, and these two had turned a team into a plan.

What do you do when a wall of thorns doesn't work? You build a bigger one. The camps put up bomas, thick fences of thorn bushes, the kind that would shred your arm if you tried to push through. They kept fires burning all night. They rattled empty tins and fired shots into the dark. For a few nights each time, it worked. Then the lions found a weak spot, or simply leapt the fence, and the whole thing started again.

And that's where it gets strange. The lions seemed to learn. When one camp became a fortress, they switched to another, sometimes miles down the line. Patterson would sit up all night in a tree above the spot they'd last struck, and they'd take someone from a camp he wasn't watching. Once they raided the hospital tent, where the sick were sleeping. Patterson moved the hospital. The lions found the old place empty, and a few nights later they found the new one.

By December, the workers had had enough. When a train came through, hundreds of them threw themselves across the tracks to stop it, climbed aboard, and left. The ones who stayed did no building at all. They spent their days making shelters: platforms on poles, beds hung in trees, holes dug in the floor of their tents and covered with logs. Some trees, by one account, held more beds than branches could reasonably carry. Work on the bridge stopped for about three weeks.

Back in London, the British Prime Minister, Lord Salisbury, later described it in the House of Lords. The whole of the works, he said, had been put to a stop because a party of man-eating lions appeared in the area. Imagine being the official who has to explain to Parliament that a railway costing millions of pounds lost a fight with two cats.

How do you hunt an animal that is clearly hunting you back? Patterson tried everything. He built a trap out of railway sleepers and iron rails, a box with two rooms: bait in one, the lion's door in the other. The bait was a group of armed men. One night a lion walked in, the door slammed, and the men inside were so frightened that they fired wildly in every direction, shot away one of the bars of the door, and the lion slipped out. Somehow nobody was hurt. That is the closest thing to comedy in this entire story.

Then, on the ninth of December, Patterson sat on a flimsy wooden platform, built on four poles, next to the body of a donkey the lions had killed. It was a pitch black night. The lion didn't go for the donkey. It began to circle the platform, slowly, for what felt to him like hours, and he understood that tonight he was the bait. Finally he caught a shape in the dark and fired. The lion bolted into the bush with a terrible roar, and by morning it was found dead a short distance away.

Measured on the ground, it was nine feet eight inches from nose to tail. Lay it down in your living room and it's longer than most sofas, with room to spare. It took eight men to carry it back to camp. The second lion lasted three more weeks. Patterson shot at it again and again over several nights, and on the twenty-ninth of December it finally fell, a few strides from the tree where he had taken shelter. The workers came back. The bridge was finished early in eighteen ninety-nine.

And here's the detail people forget. Both lions were males, and neither had a proper mane. That isn't a deformity. Male lions in Tsavo are often nearly maneless even today, probably because of the heat and the thorny bush. So the two most famous lions in history looked, to most people, like oversized lionesses.

So why did they do it? This is the question that kept scientists busy for more than a century, and the first answers were wrong. One idea was that the area was cursed with too few animals to hunt. In the eighteen nineties, a cattle plague called rinderpest swept through East Africa and killed vast numbers of buffalo and cattle. Fewer buffalo, the theory went, meant hungry lions. Another idea was darker. Tsavo sat on an old trade route, and for centuries the bodies of people who died along it were left where they fell. Lions that grew up scavenging on human remains might not see much difference between a body and a sleeping man.

Then someone actually looked inside the first lion's mouth. One of its lower canine teeth was broken, the root badly infected, and other teeth were damaged or missing. For a lion, that's a serious problem. It kills big animals like zebra and buffalo by clamping its jaws around the throat and holding on. With a broken canine, holding on hurts, and a buffalo fights back. A sleeping person, by comparison, is easy.

Is that the whole answer? Not quite. A study of the microscopic scratches on their teeth found that the lions weren't crunching bones the way a truly starving lion does. They were eating soft meat and leaving the rest. That fits an animal that found an easy option, not one at the edge of death. One lion was injured, the other was healthy, and they hunted together. And as you'll see later, when someone counted what each lion really ate, the healthy one was not the worse of the two. One lion had a reason. The other, maybe, had a teacher.

But what if the lions of Tsavo weren't a freak of history? What if there were a place where a predator did this every single year, and nobody could fix it? There is. It sits where the Ganges and the Brahmaputra pour into the Bay of Bengal, split between India and Bangladesh. It's called the Sundarbans, and it is the largest mangrove forest on Earth. Picture roughly ten thousand square kilometers of trees standing on stilt-like roots, cut into hundreds of islands by rivers and channels. Twice a day the sea floods in and the forest half disappears. Twice a day it drains out and leaves a world of grey, sucking mud.

Around two hundred tigers live in this maze, across both countries. And they share it with people. Millions of people live in the villages right on its edge, and many of them have no choice but to go into the forest for a living. Fishermen in narrow wooden boats. Crab collectors working the mud at low tide. Woodcutters. And the bravest job of all, the honey collectors, who walk deep into the mangroves every spring to find wild beehives, armed with smoke, a knife, and a prayer.

Here's the scale of it. Records kept between the late nineteen forties and the early nineteen eighties on the Indian side alone count, on average, more than twenty people killed by tigers every year. Bangladeshi records tell a similar story, and local workers will tell you the real numbers were higher, because many people enter the forest without permits and their deaths are never written down. In the villages along the edge, there are so many women who lost their husbands this way that they have a name. Tiger widows.

So why here? Why does a tiger in the Sundarbans treat people differently from a tiger almost anywhere else? Nobody has one clean answer, and that alone tells you something. But scientists keep coming back to three ideas.

The first is the tide. A tiger marks its territory with scent, spraying trees and scraping the ground, the way you'd put up a fence and a name on the gate. In the Sundarbans, the sea washes those marks away twice a day. Imagine your fence getting erased every twelve hours. Territories blur, tigers roam, and anything walking through the forest is, from the tiger's point of view, trespassing.

The second is the water. In most places, tigers drink from fresh rivers and pools. Here, much of the water is brackish, a mix of fresh and salt. One old theory says that constant salt leaves the animals uncomfortable and short-tempered. It's an idea you'll hear a lot, and the honest truth is that it has never been properly proven.

The third idea is the simplest and the most uncomfortable. Look at how people work in the Sundarbans. They bend over in the mud. They crouch in boats. They push through thick roots with their backs turned to the forest, often alone or in twos. To a tiger, a crouching human doesn't look like the tall, upright creature it has learned to avoid. It looks like a deer at a waterhole. And tigers almost always attack from behind.

Which brings us to the man who wore his face on the back of his head. If tigers attack from behind, what happens when there is no behind? In the late nineteen eighties, forest officials in West Bengal handed out masks of human faces to be worn on the back of the head, so that from any angle, the person seemed to be looking straight at you. A magazine report a couple of years later claimed that nobody wearing a mask had been killed. It sounded like the perfect trick. The honest version is messier. Some locals say the tigers figured it out within a year, and nobody ever ran a proper test. But the idea behind it was right. A tiger wants an animal that doesn't see it coming.

What if a tiger got an electric shock every time it attacked a person? In the nineteen eighties, officials actually tried it. They set up clay dummies dressed in workers' clothes, wired to car batteries, so a tiger that pounced got a nasty electric shock instead of a meal. The point was to teach tigers a lesson about the human shape. Later, long nylon nets were stretched along miles of forest edge to keep tigers from swimming across to the villages at night. In the Indian part of the forest, deaths fell compared with the worst decades, although every year there are still new ones.

What do you carry into a forest where you might be the prey? Before entering the forest, many fishermen and honey collectors, Hindu and Muslim alike, pray to Bonbibi, the lady of the forest. In the old story, the tiger is a demon king named Dakshin Rai, who wanted human flesh, and Bonbibi made a deal with him. The forest is his. People may enter it, but only with empty hands and an empty heart. Take only what you need, and he leaves you alone. Be greedy, and you're his. It's a folk tale, of course. It's also the most accurate summary of the Sundarbans I know.

Remember the number everyone repeated about Tsavo? It was wrong. For a hundred years the famous figure was one hundred and thirty-five. That's the number Patterson gave in his book, The Man-Eaters of Tsavo, published in nineteen-oh-seven: twenty-eight Indian workers, he wrote, plus scores of local Africans whose deaths were never recorded. The railway company's official records only listed twenty-eight. So who was right? A soldier with a book to sell, or a company with a reputation to protect?

The lions themselves answered. For about twenty-five years, their two skins lay as rugs on Patterson's floor. Then, in nineteen twenty-four, he sold them to the Field Museum in Chicago, and you can still see the two lions there, stuffed and standing behind glass. In two thousand nine, a team of scientists took tiny samples of their bones and hair and measured chemical signatures in them. You are what you eat, chemically speaking. Grass-eating zebras leave one signature in a lion. People, eating crops and cattle, leave another. By that measure, over their last months the injured lion ate the equivalent of about twenty-four people, and the healthy one about eleven. Around thirty-five in total. Horrifying, and about a quarter of the legend.

Then, in two thousand twenty-four, came the museum drawer. Back in the nineteen nineties, someone noticed thick clumps of compacted hair packed into the cavities of those broken teeth. Decades later, a team pulled the DNA out of them. They found human, of course. But also giraffe, zebra, oryx, waterbuck, and wildebeest, an animal that wasn't even supposed to live around Tsavo at the time. Only a single buffalo hair. So these weren't lions that ate only people. They were lions that added people to the menu.

That's why the honest number matters. A myth of one hundred and thirty-five turns two lions into monsters. The real story is scarier, because it's ordinary. A lion with a broken tooth, a partner that copied it, and a camp full of easy prey that couldn't run.

What does it take, then, for a predator to look at you and see food? We now have two very different cases, and they point to the same three things.

First, a reason. Something that makes normal prey harder to get. A broken tooth in Tsavo. A forest in the Sundarbans where the tide wipes out the rules every twelve hours and the water itself is half the sea.

Second, an opportunity. People in the wrong posture, in the wrong place, at the wrong time. Men asleep in tents behind a fence that can be jumped. People bent double in the mud with their backs to the trees. A predator that has spent millions of years learning to avoid tall, upright, noisy humans suddenly meets humans who are none of those things.

And third, a lesson. This is the part that matters most. A predator that tries a human and succeeds has learned something, and it doesn't forget. In Tsavo, the healthy lion hunted beside the injured one. In the Sundarbans, many experts believe a tigress that takes people can pass the habit on to her cubs. Man-eating isn't a species. It's a skill. And the uncomfortable truth is that in both places, it was people who set up the classroom. We built the camps in lion country. We send the poorest workers into tiger mud.

Which is also why it's so rare. Almost everywhere else, lions and tigers grow up learning that humans are dangerous, loud, and strange, and they keep away. The man-eaters are the exceptions that got taught otherwise.

Let's go back to that tent. Eighteen ninety-eight, a thorn fence, a dying fire. You now know what was on the other side of the canvas. Not a monster. A lion with a bad tooth, and its partner, who had both worked out that the tall animals become very short and very quiet when they sleep.

They're standing in a museum in Chicago now, behind glass, with the lights on. Thousands of people walk past them every year and feel perfectly safe.

They felt safe in Tsavo too.
