PROJECT CONTEXT:
Project: a 20-minute English-language wildlife documentary for adults, channel of land animals, titled "Africa's Animals Fear Your Voice More Than a Lion". Story: in the Greater Kruger in South Africa, scientists hid camera-speaker boxes at waterholes and played birdsong, lions snarling and growling, dogs and gunshots, and people calmly talking; giraffes, leopards, hyenas, warthogs, elephants and rhinos ran from the human voices more than from the lions. Earlier experiments: pumas in the Santa Cruz Mountains of California abandoned their kills when they heard people talking on the radio; badgers in Wytham Woods near Oxford stopped feeding when they heard people reading aloud. Why: humans are a "super predator" that kills prime adults and other predators. Mammals worldwide shift into the night near people. Why some animals still hunt people (the Tsavo lions of 1898, Sundarbans tigers, campsite bears), how to use your voice with pumas and bears, and a plan to steer southern white rhinos away from poachers with recorded voices. Ending teaser: Burmese pythons in the Florida Everglades.

Visual style (used for every image): cinematic wildlife documentary photograph, natural light, earthy muted palette with one warm accent (golden hour, firelight, a red recording light). The 1898 Tsavo flashback looks like an archival sepia photograph. Comparisons, maps and diagrams are naturalist engraving-style illustrations on warm parchment with one human silhouette for scale and no labels.

Recurring characters (keep identical across shots):
RHINO — a big southern white rhino bull, grey hide with red dust, long front horn, square wide lips, a notch in the left ear.
SPEAKER BOX — a small olive-green weatherproof box with a round speaker grille and a camera lens, strapped to a tree trunk with webbing.
PUMA — a lean adult puma, plain tawny coat, pale muzzle, black-tipped ears and tail.

Hard rules: 16:9, no text or letters or numbers in the image, no watermarks, no logos, no signs, no flags. No blood, no wounds, no bodies, no animals fighting or killing in close-up: hunts and kills are shown only before or after, or hidden in shadow and ferns. Animals with true-to-species anatomy, never cartoonish. Researchers, rangers, hikers and villagers are generic people, never recognisable real persons; scientists are seen from behind or in profile. Books, screens, papers and maps are always blurred and unreadable.
