# Why Were Animals Bigger In Prehistoric Times? · 391K просм. · 24.2 мин · Evolution Explained · 2026-08-19 · aoQyRSsZqlo

Why were animals so much larger in prehistoric times?

Most people imagine the prehistoric world as a place where everything was bigger. Giant insects. Giant sea scorpions. Armoured fish. Dinosaurs. Marine reptiles. Terror birds. Mammoths. Giant snakes. Massive mammals.

But the real story is more interesting than that.

Prehistoric animals did not simply get huge because the past was more extreme. Each era of giants had its own specific explanation.

In the Carboniferous, high oxygen levels allowed insects like Meganeura and Arthropleura to reach sizes that would be impossible today. In ancient oceans, the absence of major competition allowed sea scorpions like Jaekelopterus to dominate before jawed fish took over. In the Devonian, an evolutionary arms race produced Dunkleosteus, one of the most powerful armoured fish ever discovered.

Then came the dinosaurs.

For over 165 million years, dinosaurs used a unique biological toolkit: hollow bones, efficient breathing systems, rapid growth, and ecosystems filled with enormous prey. That combination produced some of the largest land animals Earth has ever seen, from T. rex and Giganotosaurus to Brachiosaurus, Argentinosaurus, and Patagotitan.

But gigantism did not stop with dinosaurs.

Warm prehistoric climates helped reptiles like Titanoboa reach impossible sizes. Ecological vacancy after the asteroid allowed terror birds to become apex predators. And once dinosaur dominance was gone, mammals finally had their own age of giants, producing mammoths, woolly rhinoceroses, giant ground sloths, Paraceratherium, and more.

This video explains why prehistoric animals got so big, why they disappeared, and why the biggest animal ever is actually still alive today: the blue whale.

The age of giants was not one era.

It was many.

And each one tells us something remarkable about the relationship between life and the planet it lives on.

# Why are there no aquatic dinosaurs? · 184K просм. · 19.6 мин · Evolution Explained · 2026-08-27 · 3hf3RQgIlFk
Теги: 
Why are there no aquatic dinosaurs?

Dinosaurs dominated Earth for around 165 million years. They ruled forests, deserts, floodplains, swamps, coastlines, and eventually even the air through their surviving descendants, birds. But for all that time, one environment remained almost completely out of reach.

The sea.

People often call mosasaurs, plesiosaurs, and ichthyosaurs “sea dinosaurs,” but that is not actually correct. These animals lived alongside dinosaurs, but they were not dinosaurs. Mosasaurs were giant marine lizards. Plesiosaurs were separate marine reptiles. Ichthyosaurs were dolphin-shaped ocean reptiles. They ruled the prehistoric oceans while true dinosaurs stayed mostly tied to land.

So why were there no full-time aquatic dinosaurs?

The answer comes down to anatomy, eggs, competition, and evolution.

Dinosaurs were built for land. Their upright legs, rigid hips, and specialised skeletons made them incredibly efficient walkers and runners, but those same features made it difficult to become fully aquatic. A dinosaur body was not shaped like a dolphin, crocodile, ichthyosaur, or whale. It was a land machine.

Then there was the bigger problem: eggs.

Dinosaur eggs needed air. Their shells were porous, allowing oxygen in and carbon dioxide out. Put those eggs underwater and the embryo could not survive. That meant every dinosaur, no matter how aquatic it became, still had to return to solid land to reproduce.

Spinosaurus came closer than any other known dinosaur. It had dense bones, a crocodile-like snout, conical teeth for catching fish, high nostrils, and a paddle-shaped tail. It likely spent huge amounts of time in rivers and lagoons, hunting fish in freshwater environments. But even Spinosaurus never became a true ocean animal.

The prehistoric seas were already ruled by animals perfectly adapted for life in water: ichthyosaurs, plesiosaurs, mosasaurs, and later marine mammals. Dinosaurs were late to a world that was already occupied.

But there is one twist.

Birds are dinosaurs. And penguins are birds.

That means, in a strange way, one dinosaur lineage eventually did conquer the ocean. Not during the age of T. rex or Spinosaurus, but long after the asteroid, when penguins evolved wings into flippers, heavy bones for diving, and bodies built for life in water.

So the real answer is not that dinosaurs never conquered the ocean.

It is that it took them until now.
