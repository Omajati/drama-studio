You're on a gravel road in Tasmania after midnight when something crosses your headlights: striped, dog-sized, tail stiff as a stick. The last known one died ninety years ago. Yet people have reported more than a thousand sightings over the last century. So are they all wrong, or is the Tasmanian tiger still out there?

Three things wait down this road: a famous film that may not show the last tiger at all, a body found eighty-six years late where nobody looked, and a study suggesting the tiger outlived its own obituary by decades.

What kind of animal has the head of a wolf, the stripes of a tiger and the pouch of a kangaroo? That's the thylacine, and almost every word people use for it is wrong. It wasn't a tiger. It wasn't a wolf. It was a marsupial, which makes it closer kin to a koala than to any dog that ever lived.

Picture one standing next to you. An adult reached roughly the weight of a border collie, often somewhere between fifteen and thirty kilograms. Nose to tail tip, a big one was about as long as a grown man is tall. The back half of the body carried a row of dark bands, usually somewhere around fifteen to twenty of them, running from the shoulders to the base of that thick, stiff tail.

And the strangest part: both females and males had a pouch. The female carried up to four young in hers, and it opened toward the back legs. The male had a partial pouch that worked like a shield while it ran through thick scrub.

Put a thylacine skull next to a wolf skull and most people can't tell them apart, yet their families split in the age of dinosaurs. Hunting on four legs kept asking the same questions, and evolution gave the same answers twice. The thylacine was also the last survivor of a whole family of marsupial hunters. On the mainland it vanished roughly three thousand years ago, likely squeezed out by dingoes, climate and people. Only Tasmania, cut off by the sea, kept it.

Within about a hundred and thirty years of Europeans arriving, it was gone. At least, officially. When the Tasmanian government released its files under a freedom of information request in twenty nineteen, they held eight new reports of thylacine sightings from just the previous three years. Not one came with a body, a hair sample or a clear photograph.

How do you wipe out an animal that most farmers rarely even saw? You put a price on its head. And in Tasmania, that price was one pound.

From eighteen eighty-eight to nineteen oh nine, the Tasmanian government paid a bounty for every dead thylacine, a full pound for an adult and ten shillings for a young one. Private companies and farming groups paid their own bounties on top. Over those twenty-one years, the government alone paid out more than two thousand one hundred and eighty times. The real number killed was certainly higher, because plenty of animals were shot and never claimed.

The charge against the thylacine was sheep. It was blamed for losses across the island's farmland, and a few famous photographs seemed to prove it. One of the best known showed a thylacine with a chicken in its jaws. Decades later, a careful analysis of the original glass negative argued that the animal in that picture was most likely a stuffed specimen, posed for the camera.

The bounties weren't the only weapon. Land was cleared for farms. Wild dogs spread. A disease that looked like distemper may have swept through the last populations. By the nineteen twenties, sightings were getting rare. In nineteen thirty, a farmer near Mawbanna in the northwest shot one in his yard and posed with the body for a photograph. It is usually called the last wild thylacine ever killed with proof.

And then the government decided to protect it. On the tenth of July, nineteen thirty-six, the thylacine was finally added to the protected list in Tasmania.

That law arrived just fifty-nine days before the last known thylacine died.

Who was the last Tasmanian tiger? For almost a century, the answer seemed simple. His name was Benjamin, he lived in the Beaumaris Zoo in Hobart, and he was caught on film.

In December of nineteen thirty-three, a young naturalist named David Fleay set up his camera inside the thylacine enclosure. What he shot became some of the most famous wildlife footage ever filmed in Australia: just under eighty seconds, less time than it takes to make a cup of tea, of a striped animal pacing its pen, sniffing the air, turning toward the lens, and opening its jaws in a huge, slow yawn. Fleay later told people he paid for those shots. While he worked under the dark cloth of his camera, the thylacine walked up behind him and bit him on the backside.

Fewer than a dozen films of living thylacines survive, and all together they add up to little more than three minutes of silent black and white. Fleay's is the longest. When you've seen a thylacine move, you've almost certainly seen that animal.

The death is just as famous. On the seventh of September, nineteen thirty-six, the last known thylacine died at the Hobart zoo. In the most repeated account, it was shut out of its sheltered den on a freezing night and left on bare concrete. Whatever the cause, by morning the species was gone from every zoo on Earth.

And that's where it gets weird. Because almost every part of that story has since come apart.

The name Benjamin first appeared in nineteen sixty-eight, more than thirty years after the death, from a man named Frank Darby who said he had been a keeper at the zoo. The zoologist Robert Paddle, who spent years in the zoo's records, could find no sign that anyone called Frank Darby had ever worked there. Darby also seems to be the source of the idea that the last thylacine was a male.

So where was the body? In twenty twenty-two, Paddle and Kathryn Medlock of the Tasmanian Museum and Art Gallery went looking for it. The remains of the last thylacine had been missing for decades. Everyone assumed they had been thrown out. Instead, the two researchers found an unpublished taxidermist's report from nineteen thirty-six and thirty-seven, which listed a thylacine among the animals worked on that year.

They followed that clue into the museum's own collection. The skin and skeleton had been there all along, catalogued as an ordinary specimen, and at one point tucked away in a cupboard used by the museum's education team. Nobody had realised what it was.

According to their research, the last thylacine was an old female. A trapper named Elias Churchill caught her in the rugged south of Tasmania and quietly sold her to the zoo in May of nineteen thirty-six. The sale was never written down, partly because the snare he used was already illegal. She lived in Hobart for only a few months.

Which means the animal in Fleay's famous film, recorded nearly three years earlier, is probably not her. The face of the last thylacine on every poster and every documentary may belong to the second to last. The real last one spent eighty-six years in a museum a short drive from the zoo where she died, and nobody knew.

If the experts couldn't find the last tiger inside their own building, how sure can anyone be about the bush? That's the question that has kept the searches going ever since.

The thylacine was declared extinct twice, and the sightings kept coming after both. The International Union for Conservation of Nature listed it as extinct in nineteen eighty-two, and the Tasmanian government followed in nineteen eighty-six, fifty years after the last known animal died, with no confirmed body and no photograph that held up.

But the reports never stopped. Hunters, bushwalkers, rangers and people driving home late kept describing the same things: the stripes on the back, the stiff tail, an odd, rocking walk. Some of them were people who knew the bush better than anyone alive.

The money came next. In late nineteen eighty-three, the American media mogul Ted Turner, in Hobart for a yacht race, offered one hundred thousand dollars for proof that the thylacine still existed. In twenty oh five, the Australian magazine The Bulletin went further and offered one point two five million Australian dollars for a live, unharmed animal. People went looking. The offer closed after a few months, and nobody claimed a cent.

Here's the myth worth killing: that all these people are either liars or fools. Most of them saw something real. The question is what.

So why, in an age of phones and trail cameras, is there still no clear picture? Tasmania is full of cameras, most of them no bigger than a paperback book. Hunters strap them to trees, farmers watch their sheds with them, and scientists use them to monitor wildlife across the island. Every year, thousands of images of quolls, devils, wallabies and foxes pass across researchers' screens. Not one has shown a thylacine that the experts accepted.

What the cameras do produce is a steady stream of near misses. In twenty seventeen, searchers released night footage of a striped animal, and Nick Mooney, a wildlife biologist and honorary curator at the Tasmanian Museum, judged it most probably a spotted-tailed quoll. In twenty twenty-one, a search group announced that its cameras had caught a whole thylacine family. Mooney's verdict: most likely pademelons, a small wallaby common all over Tasmania.

That's the trap. A blurry animal at night, half lit by an infrared flash, can look like almost anything you're hoping to see. A fox with mange shows dark patches on its back. A wallaby from the wrong angle has a thick tail. The thylacine geneticist Andrew Pask has made the point plainly: without DNA, no video can settle it.

What happens when you treat twelve hundred sightings as evidence instead of gossip? In twenty twenty-three, a team led by the ecologist Barry Brook at the University of Tasmania found out. They gathered every record they could find from nineteen ten onward, from government files, museum catalogues, old newspapers and private letters, more than twelve hundred in all. Then they scored each one for how much it could be trusted. A body counted fully. An experienced trapper counted more than a person who glimpsed something from a car.

Their models told an uncomfortable story. If you trust only physical specimens, the thylacine died out by the early nineteen forties. But once you weigh in the sightings carefully, the most likely extinction date moved far later, to somewhere between the late nineteen eighties and the early two thousands, with the last pockets probably holding out in the wild southwest. In other words, there may have been thylacines alive in the forest while the world was already writing their obituary.

Here's where it gets contested. Critics, including Pask, argued that the result leans on sightings that can never be checked. And Brook himself was blunt about the present. In interviews, he put the chance that the tiger survives today at around one percent, and said he considers it almost certainly gone.

What if the animal Tasmania hunted to death was never guilty of the crime? This year, a new study made that question much harder to ignore.

At the end of August, twenty twenty-six, a team led by the biologist Vera Weisbecker at Flinders University published a study in the journal Nature Communications. They built detailed three-dimensional models of thylacine skulls and compared them with the skulls of around sixty living and extinct meat-eating mammals: wolves, foxes, big cats, and other marsupial hunters.

The result was a skull with no living match. The front of the snout looked most like the snouts of foxes and jackals, hunters of small prey. The back of the skull looked like its marsupial relatives. And the whole skull was oversized for the body. An average thylacine weighed around seventeen kilograms, about as much as a medium dog, yet it carried a skull the size you'd expect on a predator two to four times heavier, closer to a gray wolf or a leopard.

Why so big? The team's answer is that the thylacine hunted with fast, snapping bites aimed at small and medium animals, like bandicoots and possums, and the large skull helped it absorb the shock of those snaps. What the skull doesn't look like is the head of an animal built to bring down a full-grown sheep.

And it fits a clue scientists found fifteen years ago: computer models of the jaw suggested its reputation as a sheep killer was, at best, overblown. Much of the damage blamed on it was probably done by feral dogs, and by the hard realities of farming on new land. But the new study adds the strongest picture yet of how the animal actually lived.

Hold that next to the bounty records. More than two thousand official payments, a reward on every head, a whole government programme built on the idea of a sheep killer. And the animal at the centre of it was probably spending its nights chasing bandicoots.

Can you rebuild a lost animal from a head kept in a jar? That's the bet of the people who now want to bring the thylacine back. Since twenty twenty-two, the company Colossal Biosciences, the same company behind the white wolves from our last video, has been trying to do exactly that, together with the University of Melbourne lab of Andrew Pask.

In October of twenty twenty-four, the team announced a thylacine genome they described as more than ninety-nine percent complete, built largely from a single head kept for about a hundred and ten years in a jar of ethanol.

The plan is to start from a living relative, the fat-tailed dunnart, a marsupial about the size of a mouse, and edit its cells toward a thylacine. The team has reported more than three hundred edits so far. Most recently, the company said its artificial womb had carried dunnart embryos through every major stage of development but one. Its chief executive called it the one-yard line.

Now the critics. A dunnart weighs about as much as four sugar cubes. A thylacine weighed as much as a medium dog. Kristofer Helgen of the Australian Museum has argued that editing a dunnart into something that is truly a thylacine may be an impossible task. Their family lines split apart tens of millions of years ago. And even if a striped pup is born one day, it would be raised by people, with no mother to teach it, on an island that has changed for ninety years.

So after all of it, is the Tasmanian tiger still out there? The last known tiger, we now know, spent eighty-six years in a museum cupboard in Hobart while everyone searched the bush. But the bush still has an honest answer to give. The best numbers say the species probably held on in the wild southwest for decades after it was declared gone, possibly into the time of video recorders and personal computers. Today, the chance that a few still survive is tiny, close to one in a hundred. Close to zero. Never quite zero.

And that's the twist. If someone does see a thylacine on a Tasmanian road twenty years from now, there's a real chance it won't be a survivor at all: it'll be one that was made in a lab. The next tiger in someone's headlights could be the first of a new line, built from a jar and a mouse-sized cousin.

The people who see stripes in their headlights are seeing what a country lost, in the one shape it still recognises. And every blurry photo is a reminder that the clearest pictures we will ever have were taken in a zoo, by a man who got bitten for his trouble.
