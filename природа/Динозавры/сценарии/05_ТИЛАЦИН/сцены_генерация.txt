PROJECT CONTEXT:
Project: a 17-minute English science documentary for adults on the YouTube channel about dinosaurs and the prehistoric world, titled "The Tasmanian Tiger Was Declared Extinct. People Keep Seeing It". Story: the thylacine, a striped, dog-sized marsupial predator, the last of its family, survived only on the island of Tasmania. A government bounty (1888–1909) paid for more than 2,180 dead animals; it was protected in July 1936, 59 days before the last known thylacine died at the Hobart zoo on 7 September 1936. The famous 1933 film by naturalist David Fleay may show a different animal: in 2022 researchers found the remains of the real last thylacine, an old female, stored in the Tasmanian Museum. It was declared extinct in 1982 and 1986, but sightings never stopped: rewards, trail camera claims (most likely quolls and pademelons), a 2023 study of more than 1,200 sightings suggesting survival into the 1980s–2000s. A 2026 skull study shows it hunted small prey, not sheep. Colossal Biosciences and the University of Melbourne are trying to rebuild it from a dunnart. Opens and ends on a night road in Tasmania; teaser for a frozen Ice Age wolf pup from the Yukon.

Visual style (used for every image): painterly realism wildlife art for living thylacines and landscapes; sepia archival documentary look for the 1880s–1930s; documentary photograph look for museum, lab and field scenes; grainy night or infrared look for roads and trail cameras; clean flat scientific illustration for size comparisons, maps and family trees.

Recurring characters (keep identical across shots):
THYLACINE — Thylacinus cynocephalus: a lean, dog-sized marsupial about the size of a medium dog, short sandy-brown to yellow-brown coat, about fifteen to twenty dark brown stripes only across the back half of the body, the rump and the base of the tail; a long wolf-like head with a pale muzzle, short rounded upright ears, dark eyes; a thick, stiff, tapering tail that blends smoothly into the body; slightly sloping back. Never a big cat, never a giant, never a dog with stripes painted on.
OLD FEMALE THYLACINE — the same animal, older, thinner, with a greying muzzle and a worn, duller coat.
NIGHT ROAD — a narrow gravel road through tall Tasmanian eucalyptus bush and tree ferns, seen at night in car headlights.
ZOO ENCLOSURE — a small 1930s zoo pen: wire mesh fence, bare concrete floor, small wooden shelter hut with a door.
DUNNART — fat-tailed dunnart: a tiny mouse-sized marsupial with grey-brown fur, large dark eyes, pointed snout and a thick carrot-shaped tail.
Scientists, farmers, trappers and searchers are generic figures seen from behind or with faces out of frame.

Hard rules: 16:9, no text or letters or numbers in the image, no watermarks, no logos, no company names, no magazine titles, no signs, no flags, no blood, no wounds, no fighting, no dead animal bodies (museum skeletons, skins and taxidermy only), no cartoon style, no recognisable real people. Screens, papers, ledgers, magazine covers and diagrams are always blurred and unreadable. Animal welfare: animals look calm; hunting is shown without contact or injury.
