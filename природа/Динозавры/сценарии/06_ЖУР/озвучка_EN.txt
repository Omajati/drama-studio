A gold miner in the Yukon blasts a wall of frozen mud with a water cannon, and the mud gives him back a face. A tiny snout. Two small ears. It looks like a puppy that dozed off last night. She's been lying there for about fifty-seven thousand years.

So how does a puppy stay whole that long, and why was she alone?

Three clues: a dinner nobody expects from a wolf, two lion cubs side by side who never met, and two Ice Age puppies a new study unmasked.

The man who found her thought he was looking at somebody's dead dog. In the Klondike, the gold lies under a lid of frozen mud packed with the Ice Age. To reach the gravel, placer miners blast the muck with jets of water and let it thaw and wash away. Out come mammoth teeth, horse bones, bison skulls, and miners who spot something unusual stop and call the territory's palaeontologist.

That's what happened in July of twenty sixteen, on a small creek outside Dawson City with a name that sounds made up for a movie: Last Chance Creek. The miner was Neil Loveless, and the shape that slid out of his wall was small enough to lie across a person's two hands.

The Yukon palaeontologist Grant Zazula later took the study on with Julie Meachen of Des Moines University. Their verdict was blunt: this was the most complete mummy of an Ice Age gray wolf anyone has ever found. Her eyes were gone. Nearly everything else was still there: skin, fur, paws and tail.

The word mummy can mislead you here. Nobody wrapped her or dried her in a desert. And when people hear frozen, they picture a creature trapped in a clear block of ice, like a fly in amber. Her freezer was dirt: frozen silt, the fine, dark muck of the Klondike valleys, which kept her cold, dark and away from air for tens of thousands of years. Think of it as the slowest freezer on Earth, and one that was never switched off.

The local First Nation, the Tr'ondëk Hwëch'in, gave her a name in a ceremony in Dawson. In their Hän language, Zhùr simply means wolf.

When Zhùr was born, there were still Neanderthals walking around Europe. Her team checked that age from three directions: radiocarbon dating, her own DNA, and the chemistry locked into the enamel of her teeth. All three pointed to roughly fifty-six to fifty-seven thousand years ago.

If you saw our video about the human footprints at White Sands, Zhùr is older than those tracks by more than thirty thousand years. So what kind of world did a wolf pup open her eyes to, that long ago?

Her world was a place scientists call Beringia. With so much of the planet's water locked in ice sheets, the sea was lower, and a wide bridge of land joined Siberia to Alaska. At its widest it ran around a thousand kilometres from north to south, roughly the length of Great Britain. Across it stretched a cold, dry grassland, the mammoth steppe, where woolly mammoths, horses, bison, caribou and cave lions lived.

And here's the strange part. Most of what we know about that world comes from bones, teeth and the odd tusk. Zhùr is something else: a whole animal from that world, with the fur still on.

She lived only seven weeks. That's what her bones say. Meachen's team X-rayed the body and looked at how far the skeleton had hardened. Young bones start soft and turn to solid bone on a fairly steady schedule. If wolf pups then grew at about the rate of domestic dogs today, Zhùr died at around six to seven weeks old.

Seven weeks is about the age when a human baby gives its first real smile. It's a short life even for a wolf. A pup that age has been toddling out of the den for a few weeks, wobbling around the entrance, chewing on whatever it can reach. It still has its milk teeth. It has seen almost nothing of the world outside: a slope of earth, a patch of sky, a few adults coming and going.

There was one more thing her body showed. According to Meachen's team, she wasn't starving. Whatever ended her life, it wasn't a slow decline. And that changes the question. A healthy pup, at home in her own den, had every reason to keep living. Something happened to her, and it happened fast.

In a land full of mammoths, horses and bison, Zhùr's family was living on fish. That's the dinner nobody expects from a wolf, and it came out of a lab in Alaska.

Every meal leaves a trace. The atoms in what an animal eats end up in its hair and teeth, and different foods carry slightly different chemical signatures. Matthew Wooller, who runs the stable isotope facility at the University of Alaska Fairbanks, worked on Zhùr's samples with the team. Grass eaters on the steppe leave one signature. Food from rivers leaves another. Zhùr's hair and tooth enamel leaned hard toward the river.

The most likely source is salmon. Every year, salmon fight their way up the Yukon's rivers to spawn, and the Klondike River, which gathers the water of these very creeks, still has a run of Chinook salmon today. Chinook are the biggest of all the Pacific salmon. A large one can be longer than your arm. For a family of wolves, a river packed with fish that size is a buffet that shows up every summer.

That's a surprise, because the wolves of Ice Age Beringia have a reputation. Their skulls, found all over the region, tend to be heavy, with strong jaws and big teeth, the kind of kit you'd want for tackling horses, bison and even young mammoths. Scientists had drawn them as big-game specialists, and for many of them that's probably true.

Zhùr adds a twist to that picture. At least some Ice Age wolves were flexible enough to turn to the river when the fish came in. Wolves still do it now. On the coasts of British Columbia and Alaska, gray wolves wade into streams and pull out salmon, and some have even learned to hunt along the shore. Zhùr's family seems to have found the same trick tens of thousands of years earlier.

Hold on to that diet. It's going to matter again before the end.

The roof came down on her. That's the team's best explanation for how Zhùr died, and it explains almost everything else about her.

Wolves raise their pups in dens, often dug into soft banks of earth, with a narrow opening that keeps most large predators out. The ground of the Klondike is fine silt. When it collapses, it falls all at once. Meachen's reading is that Zhùr was inside her den when the earth gave way, and that she died almost instantly.

That's grim, and it's also the reason we can see her at all. When an animal dies in the open, the clock starts right away. Scavengers find it. Insects find it. Sun, rain and air go to work. Within weeks there's usually nothing left but bone. Zhùr skipped every one of those steps. One moment she was a living pup in the dark. The next she was buried, out of reach of every scavenger, and soon locked into ground that would stay frozen for the next fifty-seven thousand years.

It's the same reason a pea from the back of your freezer can look fresh after a year. Cold slows the chemistry. Darkness and no air slow it even more. Zhùr got all three, at once, the moment she died.

But it leaves the other half of our question hanging. Why was she alone? Wolf mothers usually have several pups. If the den collapsed, where were her brothers and sisters? Where was her mother? The researchers have been honest that they don't know. Meachen has suggested that Zhùr may have been an only pup, or that the rest of the family simply wasn't in the den when it fell. All anyone knows for sure is that she was found alone.

Zhùr's family is gone from the Yukon, and DNA shows who replaced them. When the team read her own DNA, she turned out to be related to ancient wolves from Siberia, Russia and Alaska, part of the same broad population that gave rise to the gray wolves alive today.

Wolves moved back and forth across Beringia for tens of thousands of years, and her line connects the Yukon to the other side of the world. But over time, the old Beringian wolf populations in this region faded out, and the wolves that roam the Yukon now probably trace back to later arrivals. Picture a town where every old family has moved away and strangers now live in the same houses. The valley still has wolves. Zhùr's own family has been gone from it for a very long time.

She isn't the only Ice Age baby the Klondike has given back. In June of twenty twenty-two, miners on Eureka Creek, not far from where Zhùr was found, uncovered a mummified baby woolly mammoth. She was female, about a month old, with skin, hair and trunk intact, and she had died more than thirty thousand years ago. Stand her next to your kitchen counter and she'd come up to roughly the same height. The Tr'ondëk Hwëch'in elders named her Nun cho ga, which in Hän means big baby animal. She is the most complete mammoth mummy ever found in North America.

And notice the pattern. Zhùr was a pup. Nun cho ga was a calf. The best-preserved animals in the permafrost keep turning out to be babies.

Two lion cubs were found about fifteen metres apart on the same Siberian riverbank, roughly the length of a city bus. They were born around fifteen thousand years apart.

The riverbank is on the Semyuelyakh River in Yakutia, in far northeastern Russia, where people hunt for mammoth tusks in the thawing ground. In twenty seventeen, tusk hunters found a cave lion cub, a male later named Boris. The next year, in almost the same spot, they found a second one, a female named Sparta. She is one of the best-preserved Ice Age animals ever found, down to her whiskers and the fur on her paws.

At first, many assumed the two were littermates. Same place, same species, same tiny size. Then the radiocarbon results came back, published in twenty twenty-one by a team including David Stanton and Love Dalén of the Centre for Palaeogenetics in Stockholm. Boris was about forty-three thousand years old. Sparta was about twenty-eight thousand. Both had died at only one or two months old, on the same stretch of riverbank, separated by a stretch of time longer than the whole history of farming.

Siberia has more. In twenty fourteen, a hunter in Yakutia found a baby woolly rhinoceros, about seven months old by one estimate, with a coat of pale strawberry-blond hair. She was named Sasha, after the man who found her, and she had lived about thirty-four thousand years ago.

And in twenty eighteen, near the Indigirka River, came an eighteen-thousand-year-old puppy about two months old, with his whiskers, eyelashes and nose still in place. The scientists named him Dogor, a Yakut word for friend, which also happens to sound like the question he raised: dog or wolf? The first DNA tests couldn't tell. For a while he was the internet's favourite candidate for the oldest dog in the world. Then, in twenty twenty-two, a large study of ancient wolf genomes in the journal Nature placed him firmly among the wolves.

Remember that one. It's about to happen again.

Two Ice Age sisters spent a decade as famous early dogs, until a lab read their genes, their teeth and their last meal. They are the Tumat puppies, named after a village in Yakutia. One was pulled from the permafrost by mammoth ivory hunters in twenty eleven, the other dug out of the same site in twenty fifteen. They lived around fourteen thousand years ago, and both still had their dark fur.

Why would anyone call them dogs? Because of where they lay. They were found close to mammoth bones that some researchers believed had been butchered by people. If humans were cutting up mammoths nearby, the idea went, maybe these pups belonged to people. Maybe they were some of the first dogs on Earth.

Here's where it gets contested. In June of twenty twenty-five, a team led by Anne Kathrine Runge at the University of York published a study in the journal Quaternary Research. They combined the genes of the pups, the size of their teeth, the chemistry of their bones and tissue, and the DNA of what was left in their stomachs. Their conclusion: the two were sisters, about two months old, and they were wolves. Their DNA showed no special link to dogs, and their teeth were wolf-sized.

Then came the last meal. In their stomach contents, the team found the remains of a woolly rhinoceros. A rhino is a lot of animal for wolves to bring down: a full-grown woolly rhino weighed around two tonnes, about as much as a pickup truck. The authors suggest the adults of the pack may have taken a young rhino calf, and the pups were fed from it. That's a picture of wild wolves raising their young on whatever the land put in front of them, the same as Zhùr's family by the river. No human camp in sight.

Is the case closed? Not completely. The study shows these two weren't dogs. It doesn't tell us where and when dogs did first appear, and the researchers say so. The people on the other side of the argument can still point to Ice Age sites where wolves and humans clearly lived close together. But right now, for the Tumat sisters, the wolf side is winning, and it isn't close.

Zhùr, Dogor, the Tumat sisters. Every time one of these perfect pups comes out of the ground, someone hopes it's a dog. So far, each of them has turned out to be a wolf.

So how did a seven-week-old puppy stay whole for fifty-seven thousand years? She was healthy, she was home, and then the ground came down and froze around her before anything could touch her. The cold did the rest, and it never let go until a water cannon washed it away.

Why was she alone? Here's the twist hiding in that dinner. At seven weeks, a wolf pup is only just being weaned. For most of her short life, Zhùr lived on milk. So the river signal in her hair and teeth is, to a large degree, her mother's diet passed down to her. The scientists say it plainly: much of what her mother ate came from the water.

Which means the clearest thing Zhùr tells us isn't really about Zhùr. It's about a wolf we have never found. A mother who knew where the salmon ran, who went down to the river again and again to feed her pup. Maybe that's where she was when the den came down. We'll never know for sure. But the only record of her life is written in the fur of the daughter she left behind.

The pup came out of the ground. Her mother is still out there somewhere in the frozen muck of the Klondike, or long gone to dust.
